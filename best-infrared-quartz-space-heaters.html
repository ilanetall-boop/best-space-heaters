The best infrared quartz space heaters

A quartz tube glows and starts throwing heat at whatever sits in front of it before a fan-driven heater has finished warming the air around its own casing. That immediacy is the entire pitch of infrared quartz heating — and also its limit, since a beam of radiant heat only warms what it can see. We compared three quartz heaters built at three distinct feature tiers, from a simple two-setting tower to a dual-heating cabinet that adds a humidifier, to work out where each one earns its price.

What infrared quartz actually warms — and what it leaves cold

A quartz infrared heater works by running an electric current through a quartz tube wrapped around (or containing) a resistive element, which glows and radiates heat the way a toaster element or a bar heater does, just at lower intensity and spread across multiple tubes. That radiant energy travels in a straight line and is absorbed directly by whatever it hits first — your skin, a sofa cushion, a rug, a cat — and starts warming it on contact. It doesn't need to raise the temperature of the air in the room first, which is why infrared heaters have a reputation for feeling warm almost the instant they switch on: the sensation of heat and the heater turning on happen close together, because the mechanism skips a step a convection heater can't skip.

That same mechanism is also the honest limit of the category. Radiant heat only warms what's in its line of sight. Step behind a chair, sit at an angle to the unit, or occupy the far side of a room from where the heater is aimed, and the air around you hasn't been meaningfully changed — you're simply outside the beam. A ceramic or PTC fan heater is slower to feel but eventually raises the air temperature of the whole room it's in, including the parts nobody is facing. Infrared trades that slow, room-wide coverage for speed in one direction.

This also means a stated thermostat range doesn't quite measure the thing you feel. The WEWARM lists a 50–90°F thermostat, reading ambient air temperature, even though the heat you notice is radiant and hits you before the air near the sensor has caught up. Treat the thermostat as a way to stop the unit from running unattended at an extreme, not as a description of how warm you'll feel in front of it — that comes down to distance and angle, not degrees on a dial.

Mid-tier: more elements, an ECO mode and a child lock

The WEWARM Infrared Space Heater sits a clear step up in stated features: 6 quartz elements against the BLACK+DECKER's unstated count, a 50–90°F thermostat, a 12-hour timer, a handheld remote rather than app or voice control, overheat and tip-over protection, and a child lock on top of an ECO mode. That's a meaningfully longer list for $65.99 — cheaper than the simpler tower above it, which is the kind of mismatch between price and feature count that's worth flagging rather than assuming away. Tier, in this category, tracks what a listing states, not what it costs.

ECO mode on a resistive heater like this typically means the unit cycles its output to hold a target temperature rather than running at full draw continuously — trading a little of that instant-on immediacy for lower running cost over a long session. It's a reasonable mode to leave on for a heater that's going to run for hours in an occupied room, and one to turn off if you want the fastest possible warm-up in a cold room you just walked into.

The child lock is a control-panel feature, not a heat-output feature: it disables the buttons so a setting can't be bumped by accident, which matters more in a household with kids or pets than it does to the heat itself. Paired with tip-over and overheat protection, it's the kind of spec that shows up on a mid-tier unit specifically because the brand is trying to cover more households with one SKU, including ones a bare two-setting tower wasn't designed for. The outlier: dual heating plus a built-in humidifier

The DR. INFRARED HEATER DR-998 is a different kind of product rather than simply a higher tier of the same one. It pairs its infrared quartz element with a second heating element — the listing calls it "dual heating" without specifying the second element's technology, so that's as far as we'll describe it — and adds oscillation, a remote, a thermostat, wheels for moving it around a floor, and a built-in humidifier. At $145.55 it's priced well above the other two, and the feature list explains most of that gap.

The humidifier is the feature that sets it apart from almost everything else in this category. Any heating appliance that runs for hours in a closed room tends to lower the relative humidity of that room's air, simply because warmer air holds more moisture at the same absolute water content, which reads as drier to your skin and sinuses even though nothing was physically removed from the air. Building a humidifier into the cabinet is a direct answer to that specific side effect, rather than a feature bolted on for the spec sheet. It's worth noting as a convenience rather than a health claim: we're describing what a humidifier does generally, not making a promise about your specific room or symptoms.

Oscillation on this unit does real work that the other two heaters here can't do: it sweeps the quartz element's radiant beam across an arc instead of holding it fixed, which partially answers the directional limit described above by spreading "in the beam" across more of the room over time, even though any single moment still only warms whatever the heater happens to be facing. The wheels make sense once you account for the rest of the cabinet — a second heating element, a humidifier tank and an oscillation motor add real weight, and a unit this equipped is meant to be rolled between rooms rather than carried. "Energy efficient" appears in the title; we can't verify that claim beyond the stated 1,500-watt draw, so take it as marketing language rather than a tested figure. The "With Humidifier" variant is what's priced here — check the current listing if you're comparing it against a version without one.

Clearance and glow: what sits in front matters more than with a ceramic heater

Quartz infrared elements typically glow visibly while running — a warm orange line behind the safety guard, visible across a dark room — in a way a ceramic or PTC element usually doesn't. That glow isn't just cosmetic; it's a direct sign of where the radiant energy is actually being aimed, which makes clearance a slightly different question than it is for a fan heater. With a convection heater, clearance is mostly about not blocking the air intake and exhaust. With a quartz heater, it's also about not pointing a sustained beam of radiant heat at anything that shouldn't be warmed directly — a nearby curtain, a pet bed placed too close, upholstery pushed right up against the grille.

All three heaters here state overheat protection, and the WEWARM and DR-998 add tip-over protection on top of that, which matters because these shut-offs are a backstop for a fault condition, not a substitute for sensible placement. None of that protection changes where the glowing element is pointed while the unit is running normally. The basic rule holds across all three: give the heater the clearance stated in its own manual, keep it on a stable, level surface, and don't place anything — furniture, bedding, a pet, a person who's fallen asleep — directly in the beam at close range for an extended period.

The glow is also worth a thought for overnight placement. A quartz heater running in a bedroom puts out a steady orange light a ceramic tower doesn't, which some people find useful as a low night light and others find distracting enough to angle the unit away from the bed. That's a comfort preference, not a safety spec, but it's a real difference from the ceramic and oil-filled heaters elsewhere in this category.

Where infrared loses to a fan heater: an empty corner of the same room

Take the directional limit from earlier and push it to its edge case: a corner of the room nobody is sitting in. A fan-driven ceramic or PTC heater, left running long enough, eventually raises the air temperature throughout the space it's in, including a corner no one occupies — because it's heating the air itself, and air moves and mixes on its own. A quartz infrared heater, even the DR-998 with oscillation sweeping its beam across an arc, is still only warming what the beam currently touches. Point any of the three heaters here away from a corner, or simply don't aim the sweep that far, and that corner stays close to ambient temperature no matter how long the unit runs.

This isn't a flaw in these three heaters specifically — it's the fundamental tradeoff of the category versus a fan heater, and it's worth deciding on before you buy rather than after. If the goal is bringing an entire room's air up to a comfortable temperature, including parts of it you're not currently sitting in, a convection or fan-assisted heater does that job more completely, just more slowly. If the goal is feeling warm fast at one chair, one desk, or one side of a bed, quartz infrared is built specifically for that and a fan heater will feel comparatively slow to get there. Two different jobs; neither heater type does both equally well, which is also the logic behind pairing heaters instead of upgrading to one bigger unit if a single room genuinely needs both kinds of coverage.

Frequently asked questions

Does an infrared quartz heater warm the room, or just me?

Mostly you, or whatever else sits directly in front of it. A quartz element radiates heat in a straight line and warms whatever that beam hits first — skin, furniture, a rug — without needing to heat the surrounding air the way a fan-driven ceramic heater does. That's why it feels warm almost immediately, and also why anything outside the beam, including an empty corner of the same room, stays close to ambient temperature no matter how long the heater runs.

What's actually different between the entry-level and mid-tier heaters here?

Mostly the control panel, not the core heat. The BLACK+DECKER BHTI06 offers an adjustable thermostat and 2 settings and nothing more. The WEWARM adds a stated 6 quartz elements, a 12-hour timer, a remote, a child lock and an ECO mode for the same underlying infrared quartz technology — and it's priced lower than the simpler tower, so a longer feature list here doesn't necessarily mean a higher price.

Why does the DR-998 include a humidifier?

Running any heater for hours in a closed room tends to lower the room's relative humidity, since warmer air can hold more moisture at the same absolute water content, which reads as drier even though nothing has been removed from the air. A built-in humidifier is a direct answer to that specific effect rather than an unrelated bonus feature, which is why it's paired with the DR-998's longer runtime profile rather than the simpler, shorter-session heaters above it.

Is the visible glow from a quartz heater a safety concern?

The glow itself is normal operation, not a fault — it's a direct sign of where the element is radiating heat. It does mean clearance is a slightly different question than with a ceramic heater: keep curtains, bedding, pets and furniture out of the direct beam at close range, follow the clearance distance in the unit's manual, and treat the overheat and tip-over protection every heater here states as a backstop for a fault condition, not a reason to skip sensible placement.
