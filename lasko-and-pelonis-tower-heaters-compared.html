Lasko and PELONIS: how two legacy towers compare

Lasko has sold electric fans and heaters under its own name for more than a century, and PELONIS has built its catalogue on the same kind of plain, no-frills home-comfort appliances for decades. Neither brand needed Wi-Fi, a phone app or a smart-home skill to keep tower heaters on the shelf this long. We compared three current models — a stripped-down 16-inch console, Lasko's own 24-inch digital tower, and PELONIS's 30-inch entry — to see exactly what the extra height, the extra controls and the extra forty dollars actually buy.

Quick answer

Lasko's 16-inch CT16450 console ($37.99) is the plainest of the three: two heat settings, widespread oscillation and overheat protection, with nothing to program and nothing to pair. Lasko's own 24-inch Ellipse CT24950 ($74.67) is the upgrade from inside the same company — a digital display, a thermostat, a remote and a timer stacked onto the same 1,500-watt element. PELONIS's 30-inch tower ($64.98) matches most of that feature list, adds six more inches of height and a stated 75-degree oscillation, and undercuts Lasko's own digital model by roughly ten dollars.

None of the three connects to a phone, a router or a voice assistant — see why that's a feature of the category these two brands built, not a gap either one forgot to fill.

Two brands that predate the smart-heater wave

Lasko has made fans and heaters under its own name since 1906, which puts the company in business for well over a century before "Wi-Fi enabled" or "works with Alexa" appeared on a space heater box. PELONIS has built its reputation the same way, on small kitchen and home-comfort appliances sold on function and price rather than connectivity. Both brands are still selling tower heaters today, and neither one has rebuilt its lineup around an app the way some newer entrants in this category have.

That matters for what you're actually comparing here. These are not three generations of the same smart product with different feature tiers unlocked by a subscription or a firmware update. They're three physical control schemes — a manual console, a digital tower with a remote, and a second digital tower with a bit more height — built by companies that have been refining the same basic tower-heater format for a long time, long before "smart" was a selling point in this category at all.

The practical result is a comparison that's really about hardware and controls you can see and touch, not about software. There's no account to create, no app to update, and no cloud service that could stop working in five years and leave a perfectly good heater's remote features useless. A basic console at 16 inches versus a 24-inch digital display

Start with the plainest of the three, because it sets the baseline for everything the other two add. Lasko's CT16450 is a 16-inch (stated as 15.7 inches) ceramic console heater with exactly two heat settings and widespread oscillation. Its own listing doesn't mention a timer, a remote or a thermostat dial of any kind — just a console you switch on, pick one of two heat levels, and switch off yourself when you're done. Lasko states overheat protection on this model specifically, which is the one safety feature we can confirm for it beyond the basic on/off switches.

Lasko's own Ellipse CT24950 is a different kind of product wearing the same brand name. At 24 inches tall, it adds a digital display, a thermostat, wide oscillation, a remote and a timer — five features the 16-inch console doesn't list at all. It also specifies a tip-over switch, which the console's listing does not call out by that name. In other words, the jump from the 16-inch model to the 24-inch model isn't really about height first; it's Lasko selling two different tiers of the same ceramic tower heater, one built to be adjusted by hand at the unit and one built to be set, scheduled by its own thermostat and left alone.

Both run on the same 1,500-watt ceramic element, which Lasko states on both listings. That's worth repeating because it's easy to assume the pricier, taller model with the digital display must also put out more heat. It doesn't. The wattage ceiling on a standard US household circuit applies to both heaters equally; what changes between them is how precisely you can hold a temperature and how little you have to walk over and touch the unit to do it.

What PELONIS's extra six inches and wider oscillation add

Set the 16-inch console aside and compare the two digital towers directly, because that's where the more interesting trade-off sits. PELONIS's 30-inch tower is six inches taller than Lasko's own Ellipse, and its listing states a specific 75-degree oscillation figure — a number Lasko's Ellipse listing doesn't give for its own "wide oscillation," describing the sweep in general terms rather than a stated angle.

That's a meaningful gap in what each brand is willing to commit to in writing. PELONIS tells you the exact arc its tower sweeps; Lasko tells you only that the Ellipse's oscillation is wide. We won't guess at Lasko's actual angle to make a cleaner comparison — a listing that doesn't state a figure is a gap in the data, not evidence the feature is worse. What we can say honestly is that PELONIS is the only one of these three listings to put a number on its oscillation at all.

One figure PELONIS's listing does not state, by contrast, is wattage. Both Lasko models specify 1,500 watts directly in their titles; the PELONIS listing we're working from describes "effective ceramic heating technology" and "comprehensive protection" without naming a wattage figure. We're treating that as a gap rather than assuming it matches the Lasko units, even though 1,500 watts is the practical ceiling for almost any plug-in heater built for a standard US outlet. If an exact wattage number matters to your decision, check PELONIS's current listing directly rather than relying on an assumption, from us or anyone else.

Height and oscillation angle aside, PELONIS undercuts Lasko's own digital tower by close to ten dollars at the prices we have on file — $64.98 against $74.67 — while matching the Ellipse's thermostat, remote and 12-hour timer. That's the real headline of the extra six inches: more stated precision on the oscillation spec, a taller cabinet, and a lower price than the brand that's been building ceramic towers the longest.

Timer, remote and thermostat: which model has which

Laid out side by side, the control feature split is cleaner than the price tags suggest. Lasko's 16-inch console has none of the three: no stated timer, no remote, no thermostat. It's a two-setting switch and an oscillation button, full stop. Both digital towers — PELONIS's 30-inch model and Lasko's own 24-inch Ellipse — add all three: a thermostat to hold a set temperature automatically, a remote to adjust it from across the room, and a timer to shut the unit off on a schedule rather than running it until someone remembers to switch it off by hand.

PELONIS states its timer runs 12 hours and pairs it with an ECO mode, a detail that doesn't appear on either Lasko listing we have. Lasko's Ellipse states a timer and a thermostat without giving the specific hour count for the timer, the way it leaves its oscillation angle unstated. Again, that's a gap in what the listing tells us, not a claim that the Ellipse's timer is shorter or longer than PELONIS's 12 hours — check the current listing if the exact interval decides your purchase.

Height and footprint: when a taller tower actually matters

Fourteen inches separate the shortest model here from the tallest, and that difference is mostly about where the warm air leaves the cabinet, not how much heat the unit produces. A 16-inch console sits low, closer to the floor, which puts its airflow near ankle height in most rooms — fine next to a desk or a side of a bed, less useful if you want warm air reaching a seating area across the room. A 24-to-30-inch tower releases air from higher up, closer to where a person is actually sitting or standing, which is the main practical argument for choosing a taller model over a shorter one at the same wattage.

Floor footprint tells a similar story in miniature: a short console tucks under a desk or beside a nightstand with less visual presence, while a 24-to-30-inch tower needs enough floor clearance to stand upright and, in PELONIS's case, enough open space in front of it for a 75-degree sweep not to be blocked by furniture. None of the three listings states a base diameter, so we won't invent one — but taller towers of this style generally ask for a bit more clear floor around the base than a squat console does, simply to stay stable.

The honest takeaway: buy height for reach, not for heat. All three run on the same wattage ceiling that governs this entire category, and a taller cabinet doesn't move more watts through the room — it just releases the same wattage from a point closer to where people actually are. Why neither brand needed an app to stay on the shelf this long

None of these three heaters connects to Wi-Fi, pairs with a phone or answers to a voice assistant. That's not an oversight on Lasko's or PELONIS's part — it's a reflection of how long both brands have been selling this exact category of product and how little a tower heater's core job has changed in that time. A ceramic element, a fan, an oscillation motor and, on two of these three, a thermostat chip that cycles the element to hold a set temperature: that's the entire mechanism, and an app doesn't make any part of it work better.

What an app typically adds to a heater is remote scheduling from outside the room and integration with a smart-home routine — genuinely useful for some buyers, and entirely replaceable here by the physical timer and remote that PELONIS's tower and Lasko's Ellipse already include. A 12-hour timer set by hand before bed does the same job as an app-scheduled shutoff for most households, without a cloud account that could go away, an update that could break the pairing, or a router outage that could leave the heater stuck in whatever state it was last in.

That's the throughline across all three of these models, from the plainest console to the taller digital tower: Lasko and PELONIS have kept selling variations on the same mechanical formula because the formula works, and because a thermostat chip and a remote cover what most buyers actually want from a "smart" feature without the overhead of a phone app standing between a cold room and a working heater.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Lasko CT16450 console have a timer or thermostat?

No. Its listing specifies two heat settings, widespread oscillation and overheat protection, with no stated timer, remote or thermostat. It's built as a manual on/off console rather than a programmable tower, which is also why it's the least expensive of the three models compared here.

What does PELONIS's extra six inches over the Lasko Ellipse actually change?

Mainly reach and one stated spec: PELONIS's 30-inch tower releases warm air from a higher point than Lasko's 24-inch Ellipse, and PELONIS states a specific 75-degree oscillation figure where the Ellipse's listing describes its sweep only as "wide" without a number. Both still run on the same roughly 1,500-watt ceiling common to this heater category.

Why don't Lasko or PELONIS tower heaters connect to WiFi or an app?

Both brands have sold this style of ceramic tower heater for years using a thermostat chip, a timer and a physical remote to cover scheduling and temperature control, which is most of what an app-based smart heater offers anyway. Neither brand has rebuilt these specific models around phone or voice-assistant connectivity.

Which of these three heaters comes with a remote control?

PELONIS's 30-inch tower and Lasko's 24-inch Ellipse both include a remote, along with a thermostat and a timer. Lasko's 16-inch CT16450 console does not list a remote; it's adjusted by hand at the unit using its two heat settings and oscillation button.
