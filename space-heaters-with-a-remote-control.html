Space heaters with a remote control

A remote adds roughly $10–20 to a space heater's price and lets you change the setting without getting off the couch. It does not make the heater warmer, quieter or safer. We looked at what the premium actually buys, where it shows up in the build, and three heaters that do the remote well rather than as an afterthought.

Quick answer

A remote is a convenience feature, not a performance feature. It changes how you adjust a heater — from the couch instead of from the unit's own panel — and nothing about how much heat it produces, since nearly every plug-in heater sold for a standard US outlet runs at the same roughly 1,500 watts whether or not a remote ships in the box. For most buyers, the honest case for paying the $10–20 premium comes down to one thing: how often you'd actually be getting up to change the setting without it.

Our pick for most living rooms is the DREO Space Heater ($39.99), which bundles a full remote — power, mode, oscillation and the timer — into a compact tower at a price close to models that skip the remote entirely. If you want a larger, more established tower with the same control set, the Lasko 23" Ceramic Tower ($71.99) has shipped with a remote for years and has the track record to show it. Skip to when a remote is wasted money if you already suspect yours would be.

What a remote actually changes, and what it does not

Start with the plainest version of the question, because it's easy to let marketing blur it: a remote control changes where you make an adjustment, not what the heater can do. Every control a remote offers — power on/off, heat level, oscillation, the shut-off timer — already exists on the unit's own panel. The remote duplicates those same controls at a distance, typically line-of-sight infrared, so you can turn the heater down from a chair ten feet away instead of walking over and pressing a button.

That's the entire feature. A remote does not add heat output, since the DREO Space Heater and the Lasko 751320 both work within the same roughly 1,500-watt ceiling as heaters that ship without one. It does not reduce noise — a heater's fan and motor are unrelated to whether its controls are wireless. It does not improve safety beyond what the base unit already has; tip-over and overheat protection are functions of the heater's internal sensors, not the remote. If a listing implies otherwise, that's the marketing copy working harder than the feature actually does.

What the remote does change, concretely: you can lower the heat setting at eleven at night without leaving a warm bed, adjust oscillation without crossing a cold floor, or kill the power the moment you decide to leave the room, instead of after you've already stood up. For some rooms and some routines, that's a real, repeated convenience. For a heater that lives under a desk within arm's reach all day, it's a feature you paid for and will rarely touch.

The $10–20 premium, and where it shows up in the build

A remote is a small infrared receiver inside the heater's control panel, a battery-powered handheld transmitter, and the firmware to translate button presses into the same commands the physical panel already sends. None of that is expensive hardware on its own, which is part of why the premium over a comparable heater without a remote tends to land in the $10–20 range rather than $50 or more — it's a modest addition to an existing control board, not a new heating mechanism.

Where that premium is easiest to see is in heaters sold in both configurations. DREO's Space Heater, which we cover below, ships with a remote at $39.99 and sits in the same price band as comparable ceramic towers from the same category that control only from the unit. Lasko's 751320 tower likewise includes a remote as standard at $71.99, a figure that reflects the tower's size and timer feature set as much as the remote specifically — it's bundled in rather than priced as an add-on line item on that particular listing.

The practical read: on a $20–30 personal heater, a remote can feel like a disproportionate jump, because the base unit is cheap enough that $10–20 is a large percentage move. On a $70–100 tower or cabinet heater, the same dollar premium is a much smaller fraction of the price, which is one reason remotes show up more often as standard equipment once you're above roughly $60–70.

Buttons on the unit versus buttons in your hand

Every remote-equipped heater still has a full panel on the unit itself — the remote is additive, not a replacement for physical controls, and that matters the day the remote's batteries die or the remote itself goes missing under a couch cushion. A heater you can only operate by remote would be a design you'd want to avoid; every heater we cover here keeps its own onboard buttons fully functional with or without the remote present.

The difference in daily use is mostly about reach and repetition. A panel mounted on top of or on the front of the unit asks you to be within arm's length, which is fine for a heater beside a desk chair and genuinely inconvenient for a heater at the far end of a living room or across a bedroom from the bed. The remote exists specifically to close that physical gap. It doesn't make the controls more precise or add settings the panel lacks — both the DREO Space Heater and the Lasko 751320 offer the identical set of modes and timer options from either the unit or the handheld, just at a different distance.

One habit worth building either way: keep the unit's panel accessible even if you plan to live by the remote. Firmware resets, initial setup and the rare moment the remote can't be found all default back to the physical buttons, and a heater pushed into a corner specifically because "the remote reaches" can end up genuinely hard to operate the one time you need the panel.

Battery type, and the one habit owners complain about

Remote-control space heaters typically run their handheld unit on small coin or AAA-style batteries, the same category used in TV and AC remotes, chosen because the remote's power draw is tiny and infrequent compared with the heater itself. Check the specific listing for the exact battery type and whether it's included, since that detail varies by model and isn't always stated consistently across heater listings.

The recurring theme across this category isn't the battery itself — it's losing track of the remote. A small handheld unit with no cord and no fixed home is exactly the kind of object that slides under a sofa cushion, ends up in a junk drawer, or gets buried on a side table. That's an ergonomics complaint about remotes generally, not a defect specific to any one heater, and it's the most practical argument for keeping the unit's own panel in reach rather than relying on the remote as the only way to operate the heater day to day.

A second, smaller habit worth knowing before you buy: infrared remotes need a clear line of sight to the heater's receiver, the same as a television remote. A heater tucked behind a sofa arm or positioned where furniture blocks the front panel can make the remote miss intermittently — not a battery problem, a placement one. If you've bought a remote-equipped heater specifically to control it from across the room, it's worth confirming nothing sits directly between the seat and the unit's front face.

When a remote is genuinely wasted money

The clearest case against paying for a remote is distance: a heater that lives under a desk, beside a recliner, or anywhere within arm's reach all day gives you nothing to gain from a remote, since you're already close enough to touch the unit's own panel every time you'd reach for the handheld instead. For a home office heater tucked next to the chair, the $10–20 premium buys a feature that will spend most of its life in a drawer.

A second case: if the heater is going to live somewhere you rarely adjust once it's set — a hallway, a bathroom, a spot with a thermostat that holds a steady setting rather than one you tune by hand through the evening — the remote again has little to do. A real built-in thermostat that holds a temperature automatically reduces how often you'd reach for any control, physical or remote, which makes the two features complementary in theory but means a thermostat-heavy routine gets diminishing value from a remote specifically.

A remote earns its premium when

The heater sits across a room from where you actually sit, you adjust it more than once per session, or it's in a bedroom where getting up means a cold floor between you and the unit.

A remote is wasted money when

The unit is already within arm's reach — under a desk, beside a chair — or it's set once and left alone, like a hallway heater or one paired with a thermostat that holds its own setting.

There's also a reliability argument worth stating plainly, even though it's a minor one: a remote is one more battery-powered object that can go missing, die, or stop lining up with the unit's receiver, and the base heater works exactly the same with or without it. If convenience from across the room isn't something you'll actually use, the premium buys a feature with a real chance of sitting unused rather than a feature with a downside.

Frequently asked questions

Does a remote control make a space heater warmer or more efficient?

No. A remote changes where you operate the controls, not how much heat the unit produces. Nearly every plug-in space heater sold for a standard US outlet, with or without a remote, runs at roughly the same 1,500-watt ceiling. The DREO Space Heater and the Lasko 751320 both work within that same category norm; the remote adds convenience, not output.

What kind of battery does a space heater remote use?

Typically a small coin-cell or AAA-style battery, similar to a TV remote, since the remote's own power draw is minimal. Check the specific product listing for the exact battery type and whether it ships included, as this detail isn't stated consistently across every heater listing in the category.

Can I still control the heater if I lose the remote or its battery dies?

Yes. Every remote-equipped heater we cover keeps a full control panel on the unit itself, and the remote duplicates those controls rather than replacing them. Losing the remote or running down its battery means walking over to the heater to adjust it, not losing the ability to operate it.

Is it worth paying extra for a remote on a cheap heater?

It depends on where the heater lives and how often you adjust it. If it sits within arm's reach — under a desk, beside a chair — the premium buys a feature you'll rarely use. If it sits across a room or in a bedroom where getting up means crossing a cold floor, the same $10–20 buys a convenience you'll use every day.
