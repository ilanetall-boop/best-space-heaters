The best space heaters for a home office

A home office heater has exactly one job: keep one person warm at one desk. That's a smaller job than almost every space heater on the market is built for, and most of them don't admit it — they're sized, and priced, for a living room. We compared three small heaters built around that smaller job, the wattage that actually fits under a desk, and the circuit most home offices are already quietly close to the limit of.

A home office heater doesn't need to compete with a living-room heater on wattage, because it isn't doing the same job: it's warming one person sitting still at one desk, not a whole room. Heating a person, not a room

Most of the buying advice written about space heaters assumes you're trying to warm a living room, a bedroom, or an open-plan floor — the kind of job we cover in our guide to heaters for a large room. A home office is a different problem, and a smaller one. You are one person, sitting mostly still, at one fixed desk, usually for several hours at a stretch. You don't need the air in the whole room to change temperature. You need the air immediately around your hands, feet and back to change temperature, and you need it to happen fast enough that you stop reaching for a second sweater between video calls.

That distinction matters because it changes what "enough heater" looks like. A living room heater is judged on how far its heat reaches and how evenly it fills a volume of air. A desk heater is judged on how quickly it warms the six or eight cubic feet of space you're actually sitting in, and how little floor space it steals from the chair you need to roll back and forth. A compact unit that would be hopeless in a 300-square-foot living room can be the right answer under a desk, because the job it's being asked to do is an order of magnitude smaller.

All three heaters in this guide are built around that smaller job. None of them is marketed with a square-footage rating for a reason: a desk heater isn't trying to heat square footage, it's trying to heat you. That's also why none of them needs to be large — the ceramic heating element all three use responds fast, which matters more at a desk than the slow, even warmth an oil-filled radiator provides overnight in a bedroom.

750 watts is usually enough under a desk

The number that gets repeated across this category is 1,500 watts — roughly the ceiling of what a standard US wall outlet can deliver to a single plug-in heater continuously, and the figure most living-room heaters are built to. Under a desk, warming one person rather than one room, that ceiling is frequently more than the job calls for.

The BLACK+DECKER BHDC201 makes this explicit by offering three settings rather than one: fan only, 750 watts, and 1,500 watts. The existence of a 750-watt middle setting on a heater built specifically as a "personal" heater is itself the useful data point — the manufacturer is telling you that half output is a normal, expected way to run this thing, not a compromise. For one person at one desk in a room that already has some ambient heat, 750 watts is frequently the setting you'll actually leave it on, with 1,500 watts reserved for a genuinely cold morning before the rest of the house has warmed up.

The Amazon Basics Mini goes further in the other direction: it's built as a 500-watt heater with no higher setting at all, specifically for an office desk. That's a smaller draw than a hair dryer, which is the right comparison to make — it's sized for a task that's closer to "keep my hands from going cold while I type" than "heat a room." The Amazon Basics 1500W Ceramic sits at the other end of this group, running at a flat 1,500 watts with a thermostat to cycle it on and off rather than offering a lower fixed wattage, which suits a slightly larger or draftier home office where the full 1,500 watts is doing real work rather than overkill.

The practical takeaway: before assuming you need the biggest heater on the page, think about how much of that heat you can actually feel at a desk versus how much is warming air behind you that drifts toward the ceiling. For a budget under $50, as all three heaters here are, a lower wattage setting isn't a lesser product — it's frequently the more correctly sized one.

The office circuit, and why it trips at 3pm

A standard US household circuit is rated for 15 amps, which at 120 volts works out to roughly 1,800 watts of theoretical capacity, and closer to 1,500 watts of what you should actually load onto it continuously. A 1,500-watt heater on its own, on its own outlet, is within that rating. The trouble starts when the heater isn't alone on the circuit, and in a home office it rarely is.

A typical desk setup already draws a running total before the heater is even plugged in: a laptop charger, an external monitor or two, a desk lamp, a phone charger, sometimes a printer or a small space for a mini-fridge. None of those individually is large, but a monitor and a laptop charger together can draw more than a casual estimate suggests, and a printer warming up to print draws a brief spike well above its resting number. Add a 1,500-watt heater at roughly 12.5 amps to a circuit that's already carrying two or three amps of desk electronics, and you're pushing past what a 15-amp breaker is rated to carry continuously.

The "3pm" in this section's heading isn't arbitrary. It's the point in a typical workday when load on a shared office circuit tends to be highest: everything that was going to be turned on for the day is already running, a second monitor or a laptop that was on battery that morning is now plugged in and charging, and if the room has gotten colder as the afternoon goes on, the heater has likely been nudged up from 750 watts to 1,500. That's also often the point furthest from when anyone last reset a tripped breaker, so it reads as unexplained rather than as the predictable result of adding up what's actually plugged in.

Lower risk

The heater is the only thing on its circuit, or it's running on its 750-watt or 500-watt setting rather than the full 1,500. One desk's worth of electronics plus a part-power heater rarely approaches 15 amps.

Higher risk

The heater shares a circuit with a second desk, a printer, or a space heater one room over, and it's running at full 1,500 watts. Check your panel, not the wall, to know what's really sharing a breaker.

None of this means a home office heater is dangerous — all three picks here carry tip-over protection, and the two that run at 1,500 watts also carry overheat protection. It means the breaker tripping is a predictable outcome of arithmetic, not a defect, and the fix is usually either a lower wattage setting or knowing which outlets in the room actually share a circuit before you plug in. Fan-only mode: the same object in August

A heater that only heats is a seasonal object: useful for a few months, then stored in a closet until the weather turns again. The BLACK+DECKER BHDC201's fan-only setting is the detail that changes that math, because it means the exact same unit sitting under your desk in January is still doing something useful in August — moving air across your feet when the office is too warm rather than too cold.

This matters more for a home office than for most rooms in a house, because a home office is often the room with the least say over the building's heating and cooling. Central air may be set for the whole house, a shared thermostat may not reflect the temperature at your specific desk, and a room with a west-facing window or a laptop running hot all day can swing from needing heat at 8am to needing airflow by 2pm. A heater with a fan-only mode covers both ends of that swing with one object rather than two, and it's one less thing to find storage space for when the season changes.

The Amazon Basics Mini and the Amazon Basics 1500W Ceramic don't offer this — both are heat-only units, which is a reasonable trade for their price and size, but it does mean they're genuinely seasonal purchases rather than year-round desk equipment. If a fan-only mode matters to you, it's effectively a feature decision made by which of these three you buy, not something available across all of them.

Noise on a video call

None of the three heaters in this guide publish a decibel figure, and we're not going to invent one — a specific noise rating for any of them isn't data we have, so we're telling you that plainly rather than guessing a number that sounds authoritative. What we can say, based on how these heaters are built, is where the noise is likely to come from and how to reduce it, which matters more at a desk than almost anywhere else a heater gets used.

All three are ceramic heaters with an internal fan, which is the mechanism that moves warm air off the heating element and toward you — the same reason they respond faster than an oil-filled radiator, and the same reason they're not silent. A fan running continuously a foot or two from a microphone, on a desk shared with a laptop taking video calls, is a more sensitive noise environment than a living room where a heater runs across the room from anyone's ears. The fix most people land on isn't a quieter heater; it's running the heater on a lower setting during the call and letting the room coast on stored warmth for twenty minutes, which the 750-watt middle setting on the BHDC201 is well suited to, or muting the microphone between turns rather than between sentences.

If noise during calls is a dealbreaker rather than a minor annoyance, it's worth reading our guide to the quietest space heaters we could find published figures for — none of those are desk-sized the way the three in this guide are, but the trade-off between a fan-driven ceramic heater and a genuinely silent oil-filled radiator is the same one, just scaled up.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a 1,500-watt heater for a home office, or is that overkill?

For most desks, it's more than the job needs. A home office heater is warming one person sitting still, not a room, which is why the BLACK+DECKER BHDC201 includes a 750-watt setting and the Amazon Basics Mini runs at a flat 500 watts with no higher option at all. Reserve the full 1,500 watts for a genuinely cold morning and run lower the rest of the day.

Why does my office heater seem to trip the breaker in the afternoon rather than the morning?

Because load on a shared circuit tends to peak in the afternoon, not the morning. By 3pm a typical desk is running everything it's going to run that day — monitors, chargers, sometimes a printer — and if the heater has also been turned up as the room cooled, a 1,500-watt heater plus that accumulated load can exceed what a standard 15-amp circuit is rated to carry continuously. It's arithmetic, not a fault with the heater.

Is a fan-only setting actually useful, or is it just a marketing feature?

It's genuinely useful if you want one object at your desk year-round rather than a heater that gets packed away every spring. The BLACK+DECKER BHDC201 is the only one of our three picks with this setting, which means it also functions as a desk fan once the office gets too warm rather than too cold. The other two picks here are heat-only, which is a reasonable trade for their lower price but makes them seasonal purchases.

Will a desk heater's fan be picked up on video calls?

None of the three heaters here publish a noise rating, so we won't claim a specific decibel figure for any of them. What we can say is that all three use an internal fan to move heat, the same mechanism that makes them respond faster than a silent oil-filled radiator, and a fan a foot or two from a microphone is more noticeable than the same fan across a living room. Running on a lower wattage setting during a call, where that option exists, is the practical workaround.
