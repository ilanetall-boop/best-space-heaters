Space heaters built to look like furniture

Most space heaters are molded plastic because most space heaters are meant to be functional and nothing else. A wood or wood-look cabinet is a deliberate departure from that, and it shows up almost exclusively on one kind of heater. We compared three infrared models built around a wood-finish cabinet, what that finish does and doesn't change, and when a plain plastic tower is still the smarter buy.

All three heaters compared here are 1,500-watt infrared heaters wrapped in a wood or wood-look cabinet instead of the molded plastic shell most space heaters ship in: the EXNOS ($99.99), the Lifeplus ($97.74) and the DR. The EXNOS is built most explicitly around that promise — it states a cool-touch cabinet and a child lock, the two features that matter most once a heater looks enough like furniture that someone might forget it's hot. The Lifeplus adds a visible quartz glow behind a walnut-brown finish and a 12-hour timer. The DR-123 is the one that moves: four wheels built into a cabinet the other two leave stationary.

Why a wood cabinet shows up on infrared heaters, not ceramic ones

Open a listing for almost any sub-$60 tower heater and the shell is the same molded white or black plastic, shaped into a slim oscillating column with vents top and bottom. Open a listing for an infrared heater and the odds of finding a wood or wood-look cabinet climb noticeably. That isn't a coincidence of marketing taste; it follows from how the two heater types actually work.

A ceramic or PTC tower heater warms air inside a sealed chamber and uses a fan to push that hot air out through a front grille, which means the plastic right around the exhaust runs noticeably warmer than the rest of the housing, and the whole cabinet has to be shaped to move air efficiently rather than to resemble a piece of furniture. An infrared heater built around a quartz element works differently: the element radiates heat forward, the way a fireplace throws warmth at whoever is sitting in front of it, rather than relying on a fan to push hot air through the body of the unit. Because the radiant element does the work rather than the cabinet itself venting hot air, the sides, top and back of an infrared heater's housing can run cooler — which is what lets a manufacturer wrap that housing in wood veneer or a wood-look composite without turning the cabinet into a second heating surface. EXNOS states this directly as a "cool-touch" cabinet, which is the specification that makes a wood finish practical rather than a fire-code problem.

The shape helps too. An infrared heating element usually sits behind a single flat or gently curved face, which suits a rectangular cabinet — something closer to a nightstand or a low console than a column. That's a shape furniture already comes in. A slim oscillating tower, built to sweep air side to side, doesn't lend itself to the same silhouette. None of this means ceramic and infrared heat a room differently in total output — both are converting roughly the same wattage to heat — it means the physical form each technology takes happens to suit different materials and different rooms. The EXNOS at $99.99: cool-touch wood over a quartz element

The EXNOS states its wood cabinet directly in the product title — "Infrared Space Heater for Indoor Use with Wood Cabinet" — rather than leaving the reader to infer it from a color name, which already sets it apart from the other two heaters here. Underneath the cabinet is a 1,500-watt infrared element, and the listing pairs that with a specific set of safety features built for exactly the audience a furniture-style heater attracts: a household that wants it to blend in, including around kids or pets who might not treat it with the same caution as an obviously utilitarian appliance.

That feature list is longer than either competitor's: a stated cool-touch housing, a child lock, an ECO mode, and both overheat and tip-over shut-off, on top of a 12-hour timer, a remote and wheels for repositioning it between rooms. The child lock is worth calling out specifically — it's the one feature on this page aimed squarely at a cabinet that looks inviting enough to touch, rather than at the heating element itself. The ECO setting is a separate thermostat behavior worth understanding on its own terms, not a claim about using less electricity to make the same heat; we've covered how heaters that advertise an "energy saving" mode are usually describing a cycling thermostat rather than a genuine efficiency gain, in our breakdown of energy-saving claims.

The listing's own size/variant for the unit priced here is Small, so check the current listing for the exact footprint before assuming it matches a larger wood-cabinet heater you may have seen elsewhere — Amazon listings for this style of heater commonly carry more than one size option, and dimensions aren't a spec we have on file for this specific variant.

The Lifeplus at $97.74: the same idea, a different grain and glow

The Lifeplus takes the same quartz-behind-wood approach and arrives at a different finish: a walnut-brown cabinet rather than the EXNOS's more neutral wood tone, with the quartz element's characteristic orange glow visible through the front guard when it's running — the giveaway that distinguishes an infrared heater from a sealed convection unit at a glance, wood cabinet or not. Lifeplus pitches the unit specifically for "Large Room Basement" use, and at 1,500 watts it's drawing the same ceiling as the other two heaters here, so the room-size framing is about where you'd place it rather than a difference in raw output.

Where the Lifeplus pulls ahead on paper is the shutoff and timer combination: tip-over and overheat protection are both stated plainly in the listing, paired with a 12-hour timer — long enough to run through a full evening or overnight without needing to reset it, which matters more for a heater styled to sit in a living room or finished basement for hours at a stretch than for one that gets switched on for twenty minutes under a desk. It ships with three heat settings and a remote, the same control layout as the EXNOS, just without the child lock and ECO mode that push the EXNOS's feature list slightly further.

The variant priced here is listed as "3J" on Amazon — one of several size or color options under the same Lifeplus listing — so if a specific dimension or color matters to your room, confirm it against the current listing rather than this description, which only covers the specification Lifeplus itself publishes.

The DR. Infrared DR-123 at $89.98: dual heating and four wheels inside a wood shell

The DR-123 is the cheapest of the three and the only one built to move. Four wheels are built into the base of its cabinet, which is a genuinely different design decision from the other two heaters here — both the EXNOS and the Lifeplus are meant to be set down and left in place, while the DR-123's listing foregrounds mobility, built for a household that wants to shift the same heater between a living room in the evening and a bedroom overnight.

Its listing describes "1500W Dual Heating" rather than naming a quartz element outright, and that's worth flagging rather than smoothing over: the brand is Dr. Infrared Heater, and the company's larger cabinet heaters are commonly built around a combination of an infrared quartz tube and a PTC ceramic element working together, but this specific DR-123 listing doesn't spell out which two heating methods "dual" refers to, so we're not going to assert a mechanism the title doesn't state. What the listing does confirm is a programmable timer, a thermostat, a remote, and a cabinet finished in "Coffee" — a dark, wood-toned color that fits the same furniture-first aesthetic as the other two picks, whatever sits behind the panel generating the heat.

The DR-123 is also the only heater of the three that doesn't list tip-over or overheat shut-off in its own title or the specification we have on file. That's a gap worth checking on the current listing before buying, not a claim that the protection isn't there — manufacturers frequently build in both as standard without naming them in the title, but we'd rather tell you the gap exists than assume it's filled.

What a wood cabinet doesn't change: heat output, safety features, running cost

It's worth being explicit about what the wood panel is and isn't buying you, because it's easy to read "cabinet" and "heater" together and assume the whole unit has been upgraded. All three heaters here are rated at 1,500 watts, the practical ceiling for a plug-in heater on a standard US wall outlet — the same number you'd find on a $25 personal ceramic heater or a $240 fireplace-style stove. A wood cabinet doesn't let a heater produce more warmth per watt; a watt converts to heat at close to 100% efficiency regardless of what's wrapped around the heating element, so the EXNOS, the Lifeplus and the DR-123 are all putting out the same ceiling of heat as any other 1,500-watt unit in this catalogue.

Running cost follows directly from that wattage, not from the finish. At the US residential average of roughly 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, a 1,500-watt heater running flat out costs about 26 cents an hour, or close to $2 across an eight-hour evening — the same arithmetic whether the cabinet is plastic or veneered wood, and the same arithmetic that applies to every heater we've priced on this site. A heater holding a room at a set temperature with a thermostat, like the Lifeplus or the DR-123, cycles on and off rather than running continuously, so real-world cost is usually below that ceiling rather than at it.

Safety features follow the model, not the material. A wood cabinet doesn't inherently make a heater less safe — the EXNOS's stated cool-touch housing is proof the opposite can be true when the cabinet is engineered around it — but a wood finish also doesn't add tip-over protection, overheat shut-off or a thermal cutoff on its own. Those are specific, stated features that the EXNOS and Lifeplus both list and the DR-123's listing doesn't, independent of what any of the three cabinets are made from. Judge each heater on the shutoff and lock features it actually states, not on how convincingly it resembles a side table.

Where a plastic tower is still the more practical choice

A wood or wood-look cabinet is a genuine upgrade for a living room or a bedroom where the heater sits in view most of the day. It's a worse trade in a few specific situations, and it's worth naming them before assuming furniture-style is simply the nicer option across the board.

All three heaters here are built to be noticed rather than tucked away, which cuts against them in a garage, a laundry room, a mudroom or anywhere a heater is likely to get knocked, splashed or coated in dust — a plastic shell shrugs off that treatment in a way a wood veneer or composite panel won't over months of use. None of the three states a rating for a damp or unfinished space, and a cabinet built to look good on a living-room floor isn't the one we'd put in a utility room that doubles as a laundry area; for that use case, see the heaters we've looked at specifically for a laundry room or mudroom.

A plain plastic tower is also still the better pick if oscillation matters more to you than looks. None of the three heaters here is built as a wide-sweep oscillating tower — that format depends on a slim, air-moving column, which is a different shape from the rectangular cabinets a wood finish suits. If you want heat spread across a wider arc of a room rather than aimed from one fixed spot, a dedicated oscillating tower does that job more directly; we've compared two of the category's longest-running plastic tower brands in our look at Lasko and PELONIS tower heaters.

And a plastic shell is still the right call on price alone for a secondary heater — one for a kid's room, a home office, or a room nobody spends enough time in to care what the heater looks like. Paying for a cabinet finish makes the most sense for the one heater in the house people actually see; a wood-look heater sitting unseen in a closet or a utility room is upgrading a feature nobody's using. The reasoning is close to the one we use for recognizable heater brands that charge more for a name rather than a spec improvement — see our look at what a recognizable name adds to a heater's price. A furniture-style cabinet is paying for how the heater is perceived, not for additional heat.

Frequently asked questions

Why do infrared heaters come in wood cabinets more often than ceramic heaters do?

Because of how the heat gets out. A ceramic or PTC heater warms air inside a sealed chamber and blows it out with a fan, which means the housing near the vent runs warm and has to be shaped around airflow. An infrared heater radiates heat forward from a quartz element rather than venting hot air through the cabinet itself, so its exterior sides and back can stay cooler — cool enough, in EXNOS's case, to state a "cool-touch" cabinet outright. That lower surface temperature is what makes a wood or wood-look panel practical rather than a burn risk.

Does a wood cabinet make a space heater less safe?

Not on its own, and not automatically safer either. Safety comes from stated features — tip-over shut-off, overheat protection, a child lock — not from the material wrapped around the heating element. The EXNOS and the Lifeplus both state tip-over and overheat protection regardless of their wood finish; the DR-123's listing doesn't state either, which is a gap worth checking on the current listing, independent of its cabinet.

Does a wood-look cabinet cost more to run than a plastic one?

No. Running cost comes from wattage, not the housing. All three heaters here are 1,500 watts, which works out to roughly 26 cents an hour at the US residential average electricity rate, or close to $2 across an eight-hour evening, whether the cabinet is molded plastic or veneered wood. A thermostat that cycles the heater on and off, rather than the cabinet material, is what actually lowers real-world cost below that ceiling.

Are any of these three heaters good for a garage or laundry room?

Not the best fit. None of the three states a rating for a damp or dusty space, and a cabinet finished to look like furniture isn't the one we'd put somewhere it's likely to get knocked or splashed. A plain plastic heater built for exactly that kind of room is the more practical choice; see our picks for a laundry room or mudroom instead.
