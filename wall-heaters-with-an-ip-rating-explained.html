Quick answer

Of the three smart wall heaters compared here, only the DREO Smart Electric Wall Heater ($84.51) states a water-resistance rating — IP24 — anywhere in its listing. The GiveBest Smart Wall Heater ($80.72) and the DREO Smart Wall Heater for Bedroom ($110.49) say nothing about moisture at all, even though all three are marketed as app-controlled, wall-mounted units with broadly similar features on paper.

That doesn't make the two unrated heaters unsafe, and it doesn't make the IP24 unit a bathroom heater. IP24 specifically means protection against splashing water from any direction — not spray, not a shower, not submersion. For a bedroom, living room or home office, an unrated wall heater is a non-issue. The rating only starts to matter once the room itself introduces water, which is exactly where the silence on the other two becomes a real gap in the data rather than a footnote.

What an IP rating actually is, and why most wall heaters don't print one

IP stands for Ingress Protection, a two-digit scale defined by the international standard IEC 60529 and used worldwide to describe how well an enclosure keeps solid objects and liquids out. The first digit, 0 through 6, rates protection against solids — dust, debris, fingers, tools. The second digit, 0 through 9, rates protection against liquids, from light condensation up through high-pressure jets and full submersion. A heater that states "IP24" is making two separate, specific claims: a 2 for solids, a 4 for liquids. Neither digit describes the other, and neither describes anything the manufacturer didn't explicitly test and disclose.

Most wall heaters sold for US bedrooms and living rooms never print an IP figure, and that's not necessarily evasiveness. Getting a product formally tested against IEC 60529 and then printing the result costs money and adds a line to a spec sheet that, for a heater destined for a dry room, nobody is shopping for. A manufacturer selling a heater explicitly for bedrooms, like the DREO Smart Wall Heater in this comparison, has limited incentive to pay for and publish a rating that doesn't move units in that category. The absence of a number on the box means the test likely wasn't run and disclosed — it does not mean the product failed a test it never took.

That distinction matters because it's easy to read "no IP rating" as "this heater is less safe," when the more accurate read is "this heater's listing gives you less information on this one specific axis." Every unit sold in the US still has to clear its own baseline electrical safety testing (UL, ETL or an equivalent listing mark) regardless of whether it publishes a separate water-resistance figure. An IP rating is additional, narrower information about one specific failure mode — splashing, spraying or submersion — not a general proxy for build quality or overall safety.

IP24 specifically: what it covers, and what it very much doesn't

The DREO Smart Electric Wall Heater states IP24 directly in its own title, which already puts it ahead of the other two heaters here on disclosure alone. Breaking the number down: the "2" means the enclosure keeps out solid objects larger than about 12.5 millimeters — fingers, most tools, some airborne debris — without being fully dust-tight. The "4" means it's protected against water splashed from any direction, the kind of incidental exposure you'd get from a knocked-over glass, a child flicking water from a sink, or condensation dripping down a nearby wall.

What IP24 does not cover is everything one step up the scale. It is not rated for a direct spray or jet of water, which starts at IPX5. It is not rated for immersion, which starts at IPX7. It says nothing about a direct shower stream hitting the unit, water pooling around its base, or being mounted somewhere it could be submerged during a flood or a burst pipe. A heater rated IP24 has cleared a real, independently defined bar — it is simply a lower bar than "safe to install inside a shower enclosure," and nothing in DREO's listing claims otherwise.

DREO pairs that rating with a few other stated specifics worth taking at face value because they're in the listing, not inferred: 28 dB quiet operation, app and remote control, a digital thermostat, and ECO mode. It also states an "11.5 ft/s heating" figure, which reads as a manufacturer's own airflow or warm-up metric rather than a figure with an industry-standard definition other brands report the same way — useful as a directional claim from DREO about this specific unit, not something you can line up against a competitor's number unless that competitor publishes the same metric the same way.

The two wall heaters that say nothing about moisture — and what that silence does and doesn't mean

Set next to the IP24 unit, the GiveBest Smart Wall Heater ($80.72) and the second DREO Smart Wall Heater ($110.49) both go quiet on the exact same question. Neither listing uses the word "waterproof," "splash," "moisture" or "IP" anywhere in its title or stated specs. For a reader comparing three heaters that look similar on price and category, that's a real asymmetry in the data — but it's an asymmetry in disclosure, not a confirmed asymmetry in actual moisture resistance.

It's worth being precise about what we can and can't conclude from an absent spec. We can conclude that GiveBest and the second DREO model have not published a water-resistance claim, so there is nothing to hold either manufacturer to on that front, and nothing we're willing to invent on their behalf. We cannot conclude that either heater is uniquely vulnerable to moisture, performs worse than an IP24 unit in a splash scenario, or lacks internal design choices that happen to resist incidental moisture without a certification attached to them. An unrated product could be just as resistant as a rated one and simply never tested for it; it could also be considerably less resistant. The listing doesn't tell you which, and neither do we.

The GiveBest unit is the more useful case for seeing how this plays out, because its listing is otherwise detailed: WiFi and remote control, three heating modes, an ECO setting, an adjustable thermostat, a 24-hour timer, easy wall-mounting, a "quiet" claim, and Alexa compatibility. That's a long, specific feature list sitting right next to total silence on water resistance. The lesson isn't that GiveBest is hiding something — it's that a detailed spec sheet and a water-resistance rating are two independent axes. A manufacturer can be thorough about control features and simply never engage with IP testing at all, and most wall heaters marketed for bedrooms fall exactly into that pattern.

The second DREO model is the sparser case: its listing gives us 1,500 watts, "Smart" control, a bedroom-use framing, a white finish and a standard size — and not much else. We're not going to fill in dB figures, timer lengths or thermostat types the listing doesn't state just because its sibling product does; that would be inventing a spec rather than reporting one. What the title does make clear is the room it's built for, and a bedroom is precisely the kind of space where an IP rating was never going to be the selling point in the first place.

Features that look nearly identical on paper: app control, ECO mode, a digital thermostat

Strip out the moisture question and these three heaters converge hard on the same basic feature set, which is itself useful context: app and remote control has become close to standard even on this category's cheaper entries, the same shift we found looking at budget WiFi-controlled space heaters under $70. GiveBest states WiFi and remote control explicitly; DREO's IP24 model states "app and remote control" in nearly identical language. Neither listing for the second DREO model spells out app control beyond the word "Smart" in its title, which is a real gap in what we can confirm, not a feature we're prepared to assume away.

ECO mode shows up on both GiveBest and the IP24 DREO unit, and it's worth treating the label itself with the same caution we'd apply to any "energy saving" claim on a heater: every plug-in resistive heater converts electricity to heat at close to 100% efficiency regardless of what a mode is named, a point we went through in detail when we fact-checked "energy saving" claims across this catalogue. In practice, ECO mode on a heater like this typically means the thermostat cycles the element on and off around a target temperature instead of running the heating element continuously, which can genuinely reduce runtime and cost — but it's a cycling behavior, not a different, more efficient way of making heat. GiveBest pairs its ECO mode with a stated adjustable thermostat and a 24-hour timer; DREO's IP24 unit pairs its ECO mode with a stated digital thermostat, which at minimum suggests a numeric temperature setpoint rather than a simple low/high dial, though the listing doesn't specify display resolution or setpoint range.

Put the three side by side on disclosed features and a pattern falls out: GiveBest discloses the most individual features (modes, timer, thermostat type, voice compatibility) without a moisture rating; DREO's IP24 unit discloses a tighter, slightly more premium-leaning set (digital thermostat, quiet rating, app control) plus the one spec its competitors skip entirely; and the second DREO model discloses the least overall, trading detail for a simple bedroom-use pitch. None of that ranks the heaters against each other on quality — it ranks how much each listing is willing to tell you.

Where a wall heater without a stated rating is still a perfectly reasonable buy

It's tempting, once you understand what IP24 covers, to treat any heater without a number as the riskier choice by default. That's the wrong lesson to take from this comparison. An IP rating answers one question: what happens if this unit gets splashed? In a room where that question never comes up — a bedroom, a living room, a home office, a hallway with no plumbing nearby — the answer is simply not relevant to the purchase, and a missing rating tells you nothing about how the heater will perform at the job it's actually being asked to do.

Both the GiveBest unit and the second DREO model are, on their own listing language, pitched at exactly this kind of dry interior space. GiveBest doesn't claim a large-room or wet-location use case; the second DREO model states "Bedroom" directly in its title. Buying either one for the room it's actually marketed for means the IP question never had to be asked in the first place — the same way nobody checks a toaster's water-resistance rating before putting it on a kitchen counter away from the sink.

A missing rating is a non-issue when

The room has no plumbing, no sink, no tub or shower, and no realistic path for water to reach the unit — a bedroom, living room, den or home office being the clearest cases.

A missing rating becomes a real gap when

The install location could see a splash, spray, condensation drip or standing moisture — and the listing simply doesn't tell you whether the unit was built, or tested, to handle it.

The other honest point: a brand that discloses a rating on one model, like DREO does on its IP24 unit, isn't obligated to carry that same rating across its entire lineup, and the bedroom-focused DREO model in this comparison doesn't. Judge each listing on its own stated specs, not on what a sibling product from the same brand happens to state.

The rooms where an IP rating stops being optional

The flip side is just as real. Once a room introduces any realistic source of water, an unrated heater stops being a non-issue and becomes a genuine unknown you're installing on the wall. A half-bathroom or powder room near a sink is the obvious case; so is a laundry room or mudroom, a category we looked at directly when covering space heaters for laundry rooms and mudrooms — spaces that sit uninsulated, run damp from washing machines or wet boots, and are often left unattended with a heater running for hours. A garage, an unfinished basement prone to dampness, or a three-season porch with condensation on the windows all belong in the same category: rooms where "splash-resistant" is a meaningfully different product than "not rated at all."

It's worth being just as clear about where even IP24 stops being enough. A shower enclosure, a spot directly over a tub, or anywhere standing water could pool around the base of the unit calls for a materially higher rating than IP24 covers — and, separately, for professional installation. US electrical code treats fixed heating appliances near water sources as a specialized install for good reason: required clearances from a tub or shower, GFCI protection, and in many jurisdictions a licensed electrician rather than a DIY wall mount. A product's IP rating is one input into whether an install is safe. It is not a substitute for checking your local electrical code, and this isn't the place to guess at code requirements that vary by jurisdiction — a qualified electrician is the right authority for any wall heater going in near a bathroom's wet zone, rated or not.

For the mid-tier case — a laundry room, mudroom or garage with occasional dampness but no standing water or direct spray — an IP24-rated unit like the DREO model here is built for exactly that level of exposure, and a unit with no stated rating at all is the one where you genuinely don't know what you're getting on this specific question.

Frequently asked questions

What does an IP rating on a space heater actually mean?

IP stands for Ingress Protection, an international standard (IEC 60529) that rates how well a product resists solids and liquids using two digits. The first covers solid objects like dust and debris; the second covers liquids, on a scale from light moisture up through full submersion. DREO's IP24 wall heater states a 2 for solids and a 4 for liquids — splash resistance from any direction, specifically.

Is a wall heater rated IP24 safe to install in a bathroom?

IP24 covers splashing water, not a direct spray, a shower stream or submersion, so it is not the same thing as a shower-safe rating. Whether any wall heater belongs near a bathroom's wet zone is a question for your local electrical code and, in most cases, a licensed electrician — required clearances from a tub or shower and GFCI protection typically apply regardless of a product's IP rating. Treat this as general information, not professional electrical advice specific to your home.

Does a wall heater with no stated IP rating mean it's unsafe near moisture?

Not necessarily. No stated rating means the manufacturer hasn't tested and disclosed water resistance on that specific listing — it doesn't confirm the unit performs worse than a rated one, and it doesn't confirm it performs the same. For a dry room with no plumbing nearby, like a bedroom or living room, the missing figure simply isn't relevant to the purchase.

What's the real difference between ECO mode and a digital thermostat?

A digital thermostat, like the one DREO states on its IP24 model, sets a numeric target temperature the heater cycles around. ECO mode, found on both the GiveBest and the IP24 DREO unit, typically describes that same cycling behavior under a marketing label — the heating element still converts electricity to heat at close to 100% efficiency either way. The practical benefit is less continuous runtime, not a more efficient way of generating heat.
