The best space heaters for a bedroom

A bedroom heater has one job most living-room heaters never have to do: run for a full eight-hour night without anyone there to restart it, and do it quietly enough that it doesn't become the reason you're awake at 2 a.m. Those two requirements — duration and noise — rule out more of this category than price or wattage ever will. We looked at three heaters built around different answers to that problem: a wall-mounted unit marketed specifically for bedrooms, an oil-filled radiator with no fan at all, and a compact floor-standing budget option.

Quick answer

A bedroom heater needs to survive a full night and stay out of the way while it does it, which is a different brief than "heat a room." The DREO 16-Inch Heater ($69.99) is the budget floor-standing option, best suited to a smaller bedroom or a secondary room where price matters more than the extras.

Eight hours is the requirement, and most timers are shorter

Every other room in a house gets used for an hour or two at a stretch. A bedroom, if you're sleeping in it, gets used for roughly eight hours straight with nobody awake to notice if the heater stopped running three hours in. That single fact changes what "automatic shut-off" means for a bedroom heater compared to anywhere else in the house: a timer that's generous for an evening on the sofa can be a heater that quietly switches itself off at 1 a.m. and leaves the room cold for the rest of the night.

Across this category, shut-off timers commonly run anywhere from a couple of hours to around twelve, and the interval is almost always a specific, published number rather than something you can assume. None of the three listings we have for the heaters on this page states a shut-off-timer duration in hours, so if an overnight auto-off interval matters to your decision, check the current Amazon listing for each model directly before buying rather than assuming it.

What we can say with more confidence is how each of these three heaters behaves mechanically over a long run, which matters almost as much as a stated timer number. The De’Longhi EW7707CM is an oil-filled radiator: a sealed column of thermal oil absorbs heat from an internal element and then radiates it out slowly through the unit's fins, via natural convection rather than a fan blasting hot air. That design is inherently suited to running for long, steady stretches rather than short bursts — it's built to hold a moderate temperature over hours, not to blast a room warm in minutes and then idle. It also keeps radiating heat for a while after it's switched off, which works in your favor on a timer that ends before you wake rather than after.

The DREO Smart Wall Heater carries "Smart" in its own product name, which typically implies some form of app or voice-assistant control in this category, though the listing data we have doesn't spell out which scheduling or timer features are included — confirm that on the current listing rather than assuming it from the name alone. The DREO 16-Inch Heater's listing likewise doesn't state a wattage or timer figure; treat 1,500 watts as the reasonable default for this category unless its listing says otherwise, as covered in more depth in our breakdown of what a space heater actually costs to run.

The practical takeaway: don't shop a bedroom heater on the assumption that any unit will cover a full night unless its listing says so in writing, and where a timer isn't stated, lean toward a mechanism like an oil-filled radiator that's built for long, steady operation rather than short cycles.

Fan noise in a room you sleep in

Noise is a different problem in a bedroom than anywhere else in the house, because in a living room a fan hum competes with a television or conversation and mostly loses; in a bedroom at midnight, it's often the loudest thing in the room. The three heaters here split cleanly into two camps on this point, and the split comes from mechanism rather than marketing.

The De’Longhi EW7707CM has no fan and no motor at all. It is, by product class, mechanically silent in the way a fan-driven heater simply cannot be — there is nothing spinning inside it to generate the broadband whir that a blower produces. The honest trade, covered in more detail in our full look at the quietest space heaters we could find, is that an oil-filled radiator takes longer to bring a room up to temperature than a fan-driven unit does, because it's warming a column of liquid before that liquid can radiate heat outward rather than pushing already-hot air directly at you.

The DREO Smart Wall Heater and the DREO 16-Inch Heater are both fan-driven, and neither listing states a decibel figure we can verify. That's not unusual — no regulator requires a space-heater brand to publish how loud its own fan is, and most simply don't. It means we can't tell you how either will actually sound next to a pillow; check the current listing if a specific noise level matters to your decision, rather than assuming it from the word "smart" or "compact."

One mechanical detail worth knowing regardless of which heater you choose: on most fan-driven heaters, turning the heat setting down does not reliably turn the fan down with it. Many ceramic and PTC units run their fan at or near a fixed speed across several heat levels to keep airflow safely moving across the heating element, so a "low" setting can sound almost identical to a "high" one. If quiet matters as much as warmth, a heater's lowest heat setting is not a dependable way to get there on its own.

Where it sits: floor, wall or bedside — and the clearance each needs

A bedroom is a smaller, more cluttered room than most of the spaces a space heater gets marketed for, and where a heater physically sits changes both how safe it is and how much it gets in your way at 6 a.m. in the dark.

A wall-mounted heater, like the DREO Smart Wall Heater, is mounted above the floor and stays out of the walking path entirely — there's nothing to trip over between the bed and the door, and nothing for a pet to knock into a rug. The trade is installation: a wall heater has to go somewhere specific and usually stays there, so its position needs to be planned once rather than adjusted room to room the way a floor unit can be. It still needs clear air space around its vents and should sit away from curtains, hanging fabric or anything that could drift into contact with it.

A floor-standing heater, like the DREO 16-Inch Heater or the De’Longhi radiator, is the more familiar and flexible option — move it between rooms, reposition it seasonally, store it in summer. The cost is floor space and a cord across the room, both of which matter more in a bedroom you walk through half-asleep than in a living room you're alert in. A hard, level, non-flammable surface is the baseline requirement, with clear space on every side, not just the side facing you.

A heater that sits directly on a nightstand or bedside surface is generally the one placement to avoid with any of the three products here. None of them is marketed or sized as a bedside unit, and putting a heater within arm's reach of bedding or a stack of books shrinks the safety margin that floor or wall placement is built to preserve.

Floor-standing

Most flexible, but needs a clear radius on every side — not just the side facing the bed — and a cord routed away from a path you'll walk in the dark.

Wall-mounted

Frees the floor entirely once it's installed, but the install itself is a one-time decision — choose the spot with curtains, bedding and vent clearance in mind before mounting.

The safety habits that matter overnight

A heater running while you're awake is a different risk than one running while you're asleep next to it for eight hours. None of the three listings we have states a confirmed ETL or UL certification or a specific tip-over and overheat shut-off feature, so treat a safety certification and automatic shut-off protection as baseline questions to confirm on the current listing for whichever model you're considering, rather than assuming either applies.

A handful of placement habits apply regardless of which of these three you choose. Plug the heater directly into a wall outlet rather than an extension cord or a power strip; a heater drawing close to 1,500 watts is close to the working limit of what an extension cord is rated to carry continuously, and that's a common, preventable source of overheating unrelated to the heater's own build quality. Keep a minimum of roughly three feet of clear space on every side — bedding, curtains, clothing draped to dry and stacks of books on a nightstand all count as the kind of nearby material that clearance is meant to protect.

Specific to the wall-mounted DREO Smart Wall Heater: mount it at the height and clearance the manufacturer's instructions specify, since a fixed mount means you can't move it away from something later the way you could a floor unit. Specific to the De’Longhi or the DREO 16-Inch on the floor: route the cord away from the path between the bed and the door. And independent of which heater you buy, a working smoke detector in the room is worth confirming — a baseline habit for running any electric heater unattended through a night of sleep.

Dry air and sleep: general information, not medical advice

Running any electric space heater for hours lowers the relative humidity of the air in a closed room, because warming air increases how much moisture it can hold without raising the actual amount of water vapor present — the air effectively "dries out" relative to how humid it felt before the heat came on. This is a basic property of air, true of a furnace vent or a radiator just as much as a portable heater, and it's more noticeable overnight in a bedroom simply because you're in that one room, breathing that air, for hours without a break.

Some people notice drier air more than others — dry skin, a scratchy throat on waking, or sinuses that feel less comfortable are commonly reported experiences in heated rooms generally, not claims specific to any of the three heaters on this page. This is general information about how heated indoor air behaves, not medical advice. It isn't a reason to avoid heating a bedroom; it's a reason some people pair a heater with a humidifier, independent of which heater is running. If dry air is a persistent concern for you, that's a conversation for a doctor, not a buying guide, and the effect applies equally to all three picks here regardless of how each one generates heat — an oil-filled radiator doesn't blow air directly, but it still warms the room's existing air and lowers its relative humidity the same way a fan-driven unit does.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a bedroom space heater actually need to run?

Long enough to cover a full night's sleep, which for most people is somewhere around seven to eight hours. That's longer than many shut-off timers in this category, which commonly range from a couple of hours up to around twelve. None of the three listings we have for the heaters on this page states a specific timer duration in hours, so if an overnight auto-off interval matters to your decision, check the current listing for the exact figure rather than assuming any heater covers a full night by default.

Is an oil-filled radiator actually silent?

Mechanically, yes, for fan noise specifically. A unit like the De’Longhi EW7707CM has no fan or motor to generate the broadband whir that fan-driven heaters produce, because heat moves by natural convection off the radiator's fins rather than by a blade moving air. The trade is speed: it takes longer to bring a cold room up to temperature than a fan-driven heater does, because it's warming a column of liquid first.

Is a wall-mounted heater or a floor heater better in a bedroom?

It depends mainly on floor space and how the room is laid out. A wall-mounted heater like the DREO Smart Wall Heater keeps the floor entirely clear, which matters if there's little space beside the bed or if a half-asleep walk across the room is a real concern. A floor-standing heater is more flexible to reposition or move between rooms, but it needs a clear radius on every side and a cord kept away from the path you'll walk in the dark. Neither placement is inherently safer than the other as long as clearance and cord routing are handled properly.

Does running a space heater overnight dry out the air, and is that a problem?

Warming a room's air lowers its relative humidity, a basic property of air that applies to any heater in this category, fan-driven or not. Some people notice dry skin or a scratchy throat more than others in a heated room; this is general information about how heated air behaves, not medical advice, and many people simply pair a heater with a humidifier if dry air becomes a persistent issue. It isn't a reason to avoid heating a bedroom, and it isn't specific to any one heater on this page.
