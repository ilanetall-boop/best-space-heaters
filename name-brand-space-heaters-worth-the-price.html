What a recognizable name actually adds to a heater's price

Most of the heaters in any Amazon search come from brands invented for exactly that listing — no retail history, no catalog before this one product, no presence anywhere else. Vornado, De’Longhi and EdenPURE are the three exceptions in our own database: companies that sold heaters, or something close to them, before marketplace brands existed as a business model. We compared what $74.99, $189.95 and $272 actually buy from a name you'd recognize, and where that premium is real.

Quick answer

Three heaters, three prices, one question: what does the name on the case actually change? The Vornado MVH ($74.99) sits right in the price band where marketplace-brand heaters compete, and the name buys you a company that has made air-moving appliances since the 1940s rather than a storefront that appeared alongside the listing. The De’Longhi Dragon Digital ($189.95) is a real step up from De’Longhi's own cheaper oil-filled radiators, for a digital display and a programmable timer rather than a dial.

The EdenPURE CopperPLUS ($272) is the most expensive heater in our catalogue, built around an infrared element, a 24-setting remote and a claimed 1,000-square-foot coverage figure that deserves a second look before you buy on it alone. None of the three changes the underlying physics — a space heater converts electricity to heat at close to 100% efficiency no matter whose name is stamped on the case. What changes is control, documentation and the size of the company standing behind the plug.

Why most of this catalogue is marketplace brands you've never heard of

Spend ten minutes browsing space heaters on Amazon and a pattern shows up fast: most of the names are brands you cannot find anywhere else. A factory makes a ceramic or fan heater, a seller attaches a brandable name that reads vaguely German, Scandinavian or simply invented, and starts selling through a single Amazon listing, often with no working website beyond it and no customer-service line other than an Amazon seller account. This isn't a knock on the hardware — plenty of these heaters do the job they're built for — it describes the business model behind most of the category.

Against that backdrop, three names in our own product database have a history that predates the Amazon-first model, each in a different way. Vornado has built air-circulation products since the 1940s and still sells its own branded fans and heaters through big-box retail and its own site, not only through Amazon. De’Longhi is an Italian appliance maker whose name has been attached to oil-filled radiators, as well as espresso machines, sold through department stores for generations, long before Amazon existed as a marketplace at all. EdenPURE built its reputation on infrared heaters marketed through direct-response television and catalog sales starting in the early 2000s, a distribution model that ran in parallel to, and predates, its current Amazon listings.

That history doesn't automatically make any of the three the best heater on this page, or even the best value. What it means is that if a Vornado, a De’Longhi or an EdenPURE heater fails in year two, there's a company with a public reputation and actual customer service behind it — not guaranteed with a name that exists solely as a listing. Vornado, De’Longhi and EdenPURE aren't the only pre-Amazon names in this category: Lasko and PELONIS are two more, and we compared their tower heaters directly in a separate guide.

Vornado at $74.99: a known name, a generic price

At $74.99, the Vornado MVH Space Heater sits exactly where most marketplace-brand ceramic and fan heaters compete — the roughly $50-to-$80 band this entire category clusters around. What the listing states you get for that price: three heat settings, an adjustable thermostat, auto shut-off and tip-over protection, a feature list that reads almost identically to a dozen unbranded listings sitting next to it in search results. The listing doesn't give a wattage figure, and we're not going to invent one; like nearly every plug-in heater built for a standard US wall outlet, the reasonable assumption is that it runs at or near the roughly 1,500-watt ceiling the category is built around.

What the Vornado name adds at this price isn't a longer spec sheet — it's the assurance that the company existed before this specific listing and will likely exist after it. Vornado's identity is built around moving air rather than radiant heat, and the vortex-style grille on the front of the unit is distinct enough that if you've owned a Vornado circulator fan before, the control layout and general feel of the MVH will be immediately familiar. This particular listing is the Medium size in Vornado's range; check the current product page directly if you specifically want a different footprint, since Vornado sells variations of this model across more than one size.

The comparison the brand would probably rather you not make out loud: a generic $50 ceramic or fan heater with three heat settings and auto shut-off does close to the identical job on paper. The roughly $25 difference buys the Vornado name, not a materially different list of functions. We return to this trade-off later.

De’Longhi's Dragon line: a $189.95 radiator built around a timer

De’Longhi didn't build its reputation on space heaters broadly — it built it specifically on oil-filled radiators, a category the company has sold in one form or another for decades, long before "space heater" was a search term on Amazon. The Dragon Digital TRD40615EBK is a 1,500-watt oil-filled radiator at $189.95, finished at 16 inches wide by 10 inches deep by 26 inches tall, with a programmable timer and what De’Longhi's own listing describes as energy-saving features.

The programmable timer is the real upgrade here over a basic mechanical dial. Instead of picking a single shut-off interval and leaving the room, a programmable timer lets you schedule the heater to start warming a room before you're in it and stop after you've left — a calendar rather than a countdown. Paired with the digital display, that's a meaningfully different day-to-day experience from turning a dial and guessing at a number, even though the heating mechanism underneath — oil sealed inside metal fins, warmed by an element and radiating slowly into the room — is the same mechanism that has carried the De’Longhi name for years. For how that approach compares with ceramic and infrared heat more broadly, see our full breakdown of oil-filled radiator heaters.

$189.95 is also a real step up within De’Longhi's own range, not only above the generic competition. Other De’Longhi oil-filled radiators in our catalogue sell for considerably less than the Dragon Digital. That spread inside a single brand's own lineup is worth sitting with: the De’Longhi name by itself doesn't set the price, the feature set attached to a given model does. A buyer who wants the De’Longhi reputation without paying for the digital display and the programmable schedule has that option within the same brand, just not this specific product.

EdenPURE at $272: the most expensive heater in the catalogue

EdenPURE is the most expensive name in our entire catalogue, and the CopperPLUS at $272 runs nearly $100 above the next heater on this page. The listing states 1,500 watts, a claimed coverage area of 1,000 square feet, tip-over protection, a remote with 24 settings, and wheels built into the cabinet base for moving it between rooms.

EdenPURE built its name specifically on infrared heating, marketed for years through direct-response television rather than big-box retail shelves, and the CopperPLUS keeps that identity: an infrared element visible through a grille on the front of the cabinet, styled closer to a piece of furniture than a utility appliance. For how infrared compares with the quartz and ceramic approaches other brands take, see our guide to infrared quartz space heaters.

The 1,000-square-foot figure is the number worth treating carefully. Manufacturer square-footage ratings are typically measured under favorable conditions — a closed, single-story room, solid insulation, no major drafts — not necessarily the shape of your actual living room, a vaulted ceiling, or an open floor plan bleeding heat into a hallway. EdenPURE's listing also markets the unit as "Energy Efficient," a phrase worth reading literally rather than as a promise of lower bills: every plug-in electric heater converts nearly all of its electricity to heat regardless of brand, so "efficient" here is describing something other than raw conversion rate. We've gone through exactly what these labels do and don't mean in our fact-check of energy-saving claims.

What $272 buys beyond the infrared element: a 24-setting remote, far more granular than a basic high/low switch; wheels built in for moving it between rooms; and the Large size variant, the biggest footprint of the three heaters covered here. Whether that's worth nearly $200 more than the Vornado comes down to how much you value the remote and the cabinet's styling, versus simply wanting a room warmer.

Where the brand premium shows up on the spec sheet — and where it doesn't

Laid side by side, the pattern across all three heaters is consistent: the premium attaches to control and convenience, not to the basic act of converting electricity into heat.

Shows up in

Control granularity (EdenPURE's 24-setting remote versus a simple switch), scheduling (De’Longhi's programmable timer versus a dial), company history and support behind the product, and build details like built-in wheels and digital displays.

Doesn't show up in

The core physics — every plug-in heater converts electricity to heat at close to the same efficiency regardless of brand — the presence of baseline safety features like auto shut-off and tip-over protection, which are now standard even on unbranded $30 heaters, and stated wattage, since none of these three states a figure above the roughly 1,500-watt ceiling the whole category is built around.

None of that makes the premium meaningless. A timer that actually gets used saves more electricity than a few watts of difference ever would, and a company you can still reach in year three matters the day something breaks. It does mean the premium is best judged feature by feature, not accepted as a flat tax for a familiar logo.

When a generic $50 heater does the identical job

This mostly applies to the Vornado end of this comparison, not the other two. A basic ceramic or fan heater with a handful of heat settings, an adjustable thermostat and standard auto shut-off and tip-over protection is now common at $40 to $60 from brands nobody has heard of, and on paper the function is the same: the same roughly 1,500-watt ceiling, the same category of safety certifications, the same basic job of blowing warm air into a room. If that's the entire feature list you need, paying $74.99 for the Vornado name buys reassurance and a company you can find again next year — real things, but not functional differences. We've covered this exact budget bracket, including a few generic options that add WiFi control for less than the Vornado costs, in our guide to the cheapest WiFi-controlled heaters.

The argument gets weaker, not stronger, as the price climbs. A $50 generic heater is a reasonable substitute for a basic $75 fan heater. It is not a substitute for a $190 radiator with a programmable timer and digital display, or a $272 infrared cabinet with a 24-setting remote and built-in wheels — those two are charging for specific features a bare-bones generic model usually skips, not simply for a name.

The practical rule: when two heaters share the same mechanism and the same short list of safety features, the price difference is mostly brand and reassurance, and a generic option is a fair trade. When one heater adds a feature the other genuinely lacks — a schedule instead of a timer, two dozen remote settings instead of three buttons — the comparison stops being about the name and starts being about whether you'll use the feature.

Frequently asked questions

Does paying more for a brand-name heater mean you get more watts?

No. Nearly every plug-in space heater sold for a standard US outlet runs at or near 1,500 watts regardless of price or brand — the practical ceiling of a 15-amp household circuit. The De’Longhi Dragon Digital states 1,500 watts directly, and so does the EdenPURE CopperPLUS. The roughly $200 spread between the cheapest and most expensive heater here buys control, build details and company history, not additional heat output.

Is the $189.95 De’Longhi Dragon Digital the same heater as De’Longhi's cheaper oil-filled radiators?

No — it's a specific model within De’Longhi's oil-filled radiator range, set apart by a digital display and a programmable timer rather than a mechanical dial. De’Longhi sells other oil-filled radiators in our catalogue for considerably less, so the De’Longhi name alone doesn't set this price; the digital timer and scheduling features attached to this specific model do.

Does EdenPURE's $272 price guarantee it will heat 1,000 square feet?

Treat that figure as a manufacturer's best-case rating, not a guarantee. Square-footage claims are typically measured in a closed, single-story test room with good insulation and no major drafts — conditions that may not match an open floor plan, a vaulted ceiling or an older, drafty house. The CopperPLUS's 1,500-watt heating element doesn't change based on the number printed on the listing.

Is it ever worth paying more for a recognizable brand on a space heater?

It can be, if what you value is a company with a retail and service history that predates the listing, plus whatever specific features that company attaches to a given model — a programmable timer, a multi-setting remote, a familiar control layout. It's a weaker argument if you're comparing only core heat output and basic safety features, since auto shut-off and tip-over protection are now standard even on unbranded $30 heaters.
