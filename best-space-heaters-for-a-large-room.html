The best space heaters for a large room

A standard US wall outlet caps a plug-in heater at roughly 1,500 watts — the same ceiling whether the room is a bedroom or an open-plan great room. A heater built for a big room isn't pulling more power than one built for a small one; it's aimed, shaped or paired differently. We compared three heaters that take genuinely different approaches to that ceiling, and looked at when a square-footage rating is honest and when a second heater beats a bigger one.

Why every heater here draws the same 1,500 watts

Start with the number that doesn't change, because it explains almost everything else on this page. A standard US household circuit runs 120 volts at 15 amps, about 1,800 watts in theory. In practice, portable electric heaters sold for that outlet are built to roughly 1,500 watts, leaving headroom under UL safety testing and the rule that a device shouldn't run at more than 80% of a circuit's rated capacity for a sustained period. That figure isn't a marketing choice any one brand makes; it's close to the practical ceiling of what a single US wall outlet can deliver to a plug-in appliance, continuously, without an electrician getting involved.

The Dr Infrared Heater DR-968 states this outright: 1,500 watts is in the product's own title, and at full output it draws roughly 12.5 amps — close enough to a 15-amp circuit's limit that it wants an outlet of its own rather than one shared with a microwave or a hair dryer.

Neither the DREO 25-Inch nor the duraflame freestanding stove states a wattage figure in the listing data we have, and we're not going to invent one. What we can say honestly is that both are indoor, plug-in heaters built for the same US wall outlets as the DR-968, and nothing in either listing suggests an exception to how this category is built. Treat 1,500 watts as the safe working assumption for any plug-in space heater unless its own listing states otherwise — and treat a listing that omits wattage as a gap in the data, not a claim either way.

What this means practically: the $79.94 DREO tower and the $259.99 duraflame stove are not competing on how much heat they can put out. They're competing on cabinet size, materials, a flame-effect display, remote control and, as the next two sections cover, how that fixed wattage actually reaches the room.

What a square-footage rating is measured under — and what your room is like

The duraflame stove's own title states it: "for 1,000 Sq. Ft. Room." That's a specific, confident number, and it's worth asking what it's actually measuring before it decides a purchase.

Square-footage ratings on space heaters aren't independently audited the way a refrigerator's energy label is. They come from the manufacturer, measured under conditions chosen to be favorable: a closed, single-story room with a standard ceiling height, reasonable insulation, no major draft sources, and no competing heat loss through large windows or an open stairwell. A 1,000-square-foot rating describes that room, not necessarily yours.

Real rooms deviate from that test case in ways that matter. A vaulted ceiling adds volume the rating doesn't account for, since a taller room has more air above head height to warm before floor level feels different. An open floor plan connecting a living room to a kitchen and a hallway isn't a sealed 1,000 square feet — it's 1,000 square feet with extra doorways bleeding heat into rooms nobody is trying to warm. A finished basement fights cold coming up through the floor, which a manufacturer's test room typically does not. None of this makes the figure false. It makes it a best-case ceiling, not a guarantee — worth knowing before the box arrives rather than after.

The DREO 25-Inch takes a different approach: it's marketed for "Large Room" use, but the listing data we have doesn't include a specific square-footage number. That's an honest gap rather than an oversight — we'd rather say the figure isn't available than guess one to fill the page. If a specific square-footage commitment matters to you, check the current listing directly; we're not going to state a number we can't verify.

The practical approach: read any square-footage rating as the best case, not the expected case, then mentally discount it based on ceiling height, how open the floor plan is, which floor of the house you're on, and how many windows and doors are between the heater and the far corner of the room.

Moving air versus warming objects: the choice that decides a big room

Put the wattage question aside and there are really only two physical strategies a space heater uses, plus one hybrid of the two. A convection heater warms the air around its heating element and relies on a fan, or on warm air rising and circulating, to move that heat through the room. A radiant or infrared heater skips the air almost entirely and warms whatever sits directly in its line of sight — a person, a sofa, a rug — the way sunlight warms your skin before it warms the air around you. In a large room, which strategy a heater uses matters more than almost any other spec, because it decides whether the heat fills the room's volume or just one corner of it.

The DREO 25-Inch is built as a tall, slim tower, the format this category uses specifically to move air: a vertical fan path that pulls air in low and pushes it out across the room rather than radiating from a fixed point. That's the mechanism behind any "large room" tower heater's pitch — circulate the same 1,500 watts over more floor space, rather than concentrating it. The listing's product imagery shows a digital display and an included remote, consistent with a unit meant to be set and left running.

The Dr Infrared DR-968 is the hybrid: an infrared quartz tube and a PTC ceramic element sharing one cabinet, so it warms the air moving through the unit and the people and furniture in front of it at the same time. That combination is why it suits a room you actually sit in for hours, rather than a hallway you only pass through — it's doing two jobs other heaters on this page do separately.

The duraflame freestanding stove is closer to a pure radiant heater, built around an infrared element inside a stove-style cabinet with a 3D flame effect behind glass. It's honest about what it's for: warming the people in front of it and making the room look like it has a working fireplace, not filling an entire open-plan floor with uniform warm air. The 1,000-square-foot rating on the box describes the room it could theoretically heat under favorable conditions; owning it feels closer to a very good seating-area heater with a convincing flame effect.

None of this makes one mechanism better than another in the abstract. It makes them suited to different large rooms: a tower for a room you want evenly warm end to end, a hybrid for a single room you live in, and a radiant stove for a seating zone you want warm and want to look at.

When one heater is the wrong answer and two is the right one

Here's the piece of the 1,500-watt ceiling most buyers don't think through until the heater is already running: there is no bigger plug-in heater to buy. Unlike a furnace, where a bigger room genuinely gets a bigger unit, a portable electric heater sold for a standard US outlet tops out at roughly 1,500 watts almost without exception, for the circuit reasons covered earlier. Once you own a 1,500-watt heater, whichever one it is, you own the ceiling. "Buy the biggest one" isn't a real upgrade path here the way it is in most categories.

A single heater also has an effective range, shorter than the square-footage rating on the box suggests. An infrared heater is directional by design — the far side of a sofa, or anything outside its line of sight, stays close to room temperature no matter how long it runs. A convection tower fills more of the room's air volume, but still warms the air nearest it first, so the half of a large room farthest from the unit is the last part to feel any different. Put the heater at one end of a genuinely large room, with seating at the other, and a single unit frequently cannot do justice to both.

One heater is enough when

The room is a single enclosed space, the seating is within roughly 10–12 feet of the unit, and you mainly need one zone — a bedroom, a home office, or a living room you're not trying to heat corner to corner.

Two heaters beat one bigger heater when

The floor plan is open, there are two seating areas, or the room is long enough that one heater's output genuinely can't reach both ends — because a bigger single unit isn't an option at this wattage ceiling.

This is where pairing two heaters, rather than searching for one mythical "large room" unit that doesn't exist at this price or wattage, is the more honest answer. Put a hybrid or radiant unit like the DR-968 where people actually sit, and a convection tower like the DREO 25-Inch at the far end or near a doorway, to keep air moving rather than letting it pool cold near an entrance. Combined, that's 3,000 watts genuinely distributed across a room the way one 1,500-watt unit, however it's marketed, cannot manage alone.

The catch runs the same wattage math the other direction. Two 1,500-watt heaters draw roughly 25 amps combined — well past what a single 15-amp residential circuit is rated to carry. Running both from outlets on the same circuit, or the same power strip, is how breakers trip. Before committing to a two-heater setup, check which outlets in your home actually share a breaker; your electrical panel is the authority here, not a guess based on which walls the outlets are on.

Frequently asked questions

Does wattage even matter when choosing a heater for a big room?

Less than you'd think. Virtually every plug-in electric space heater sold for a standard US outlet runs at roughly 1,500 watts, the practical ceiling of a 15-amp residential circuit. Dr Infrared states this directly for the DR-968; it's the reasonable assumption for any similar indoor plug-in heater unless its listing says otherwise. The real differences come from how that fixed wattage is delivered — moving air, aiming infrared, or both — not from one heater producing more heat than another.

What does a manufacturer's square-footage rating actually measure?

It's typically measured in a closed, single-story test room with standard ceiling height, good insulation and no major drafts — favorable conditions, not conditions guaranteed to match your home. A vaulted ceiling, an open floor plan or extra doorways will pull real-world performance below the rated figure. Treat any square-footage claim, including the duraflame's 1,000-square-foot rating here, as a best-case ceiling rather than a promise.

Should I buy one large heater or two smaller ones for a big room?

Often two, because "one bigger heater" isn't really available at this wattage ceiling — nearly every plug-in unit tops out around 1,500 watts regardless of price. If the room is open-plan or has two seating areas, pairing a radiant or hybrid heater like the DR-968 where people sit with a convection tower like the DREO 25-Inch at the far end covers more of the room than one unit alone, as long as both run on separate circuits.

Will running two heaters at once trip a breaker?

It can, if both are plugged into outlets on the same circuit. Two 1,500-watt heaters draw roughly 25 amps combined, well past a standard 15-amp circuit's rating. Before setting up a two-heater arrangement, check which outlets actually share a breaker with your home's panel, rather than assuming separate walls mean separate circuits.
