The best space heaters for an RV or tiny room

A full-size tower that looks modest in a living room is a different object in a camper van: it eats the one open counter, it hangs off a 15-amp hookup with far less margin than a house circuit, and it has to survive being packed away every time you break camp. Why a tiny room is a different brief than a small room

"Small room" and "tiny room" sound like a difference of degree. On a heater-shopping list they're a difference of kind. A small bedroom in a house still has a wall outlet on a 15- or 20-amp household circuit, a closet to store the heater in during summer, and enough floor or counter space that a 10-inch-wide tower barely registers. A camper van, a truck bed slide-in, a travel trailer bunk or a backyard tiny house has none of those things as a given: the available surface is a single counter that also holds a stove top or a sink, storage is a cabinet already full of cookware, and the outlet may run off a 15-amp shore-power pedestal shared with the refrigerator, the water heater and whatever the neighboring site is running.

That's the actual brief behind this page. We're not comparing three heaters that happen to be small. We're comparing them against a different set of constraints — pack size, weight, and what they do to a circuit with less headroom than a house's — because those are the variables that decide whether a heater earns its spot in a rig with no spare space, not a square-footage number built for a house.

Shore power and a 15-amp hookup: the same arithmetic, less margin

The arithmetic that governs any plug-in heater doesn't change in an RV: watts divided by volts gives you amps, and a standard 120-volt circuit is commonly rated 15 amps. A 1,500-watt heater draws roughly 12.5 amps on its own — close to that limit even in a house. The Amazon Basics Mini, at a stated 500 watts, draws closer to 4.2 amps, which is the entire point of choosing a lower-wattage unit for a rig: it leaves far more of a shared circuit's capacity for everything else plugged into it.

Neither DREO listing in our data states a wattage figure. Like most plug-in space heaters sold for a standard US outlet, the reasonable working assumption is that they run at or near the common 1,500-watt ceiling unless the listing says otherwise — and we'd rather say that plainly than invent a number to fill the gap. If the exact wattage of a specific heater decides whether it's safe on your setup, confirm it on the current listing before buying, because the difference between a 500-watt mini and a 1,500-watt unit is the difference between four amps and twelve.

What makes a campsite hookup different from a house circuit isn't the physics — it's the margin. A 30-amp RV shore-power connection splits into a single 120-volt leg that, once the air conditioner, the water heater and the refrigerator are already drawing from it, can leave surprisingly little headroom for a 1,500-watt heater. Boondocking on a generator or an inverter shrinks that margin further: a small gas generator rated around 2,000 watts can be entirely consumed by one full-size heater. A 50-amp connection with two separate 120-volt legs has more room, but it's still worth knowing which outlets share a leg before running a heater and the microwave at once.

A smaller heater makes sense when

You're on a 15- or 30-amp hookup, running other appliances off the same circuit, or relying on a generator or inverter with a hard wattage ceiling. The Amazon Basics Mini's roughly 4-amp draw leaves room for almost everything else in the rig.

A full-size unit's assumed 1,500 watts matters when

You're on a dedicated outlet, a 50-amp connection with real headroom, or shore power at a developed site rather than a generator. Confirm the specific heater's wattage on its listing before relying on it.

The practical habit either way: plug a space heater directly into a wall outlet, never a daisy-chained power strip or an extension cord rated for light duty, and check what else is drawing from the same circuit before you leave it running unattended — the same rule that applies in a house, with a lot less slack to absorb a mistake.

Storing it between trips

A heater that lives in a cabinet eight or ten months a year gets treated differently than one that sits in a living room corner year-round, and that changes what's worth checking before it goes back into storage and again before the first cold trip of the season.

  • Let it cool fully before packing it away. A ceramic element holds heat for a few minutes after it's switched off; give it time before it goes into a cabinet next to anything flammable, including its own cord.
  • Coil the cord loosely, not tightly wound around the body. A cord wound tight around a hot housing, trip after trip, is a common way insulation cracks years before the heating element fails.
  • Check the vents for dust before the first use each season. A cabinet that also stores blankets or paper goods tends to leave lint near an intake grille, and a partially blocked vent is a common reason a small heater runs hotter than it should.
  • Keep it dry. None of the three heaters on this page is rated for bathroom, outdoor or high-humidity use. A rig with window condensation in cold weather is not a safe storage spot if moisture can reach it directly.
  • Test it on household power before the trip, not after you've set up camp. A known-good outlet at home confirms it survived the off-season before you're relying on it with no backup heat source.

Frequently asked questions

What makes an RV or camper heater different from a regular small-room heater?

Mainly the constraints around it, not the heating itself. A house bedroom has a dedicated closet, a household circuit with normal headroom, and enough floor space that heater size barely matters. An RV or camper has none of those as a given — a single shared counter, a shore-power or generator circuit with much less spare capacity, and storage that has to be packed and unpacked every trip. That's why weight and footprint matter as much as, or more than, heat output when choosing one.

Does a travel heater need to be lighter than a home heater?

Not strictly, but it helps. The Amazon Basics Mini's stated 1.4-pound weight and compact footprint make it easy to store in a cabinet and move around without it feeling like dead weight. A heavier unit isn't unsafe to travel with, but it takes up a fixed spot rather than tucking into whatever gap is available, which matters more in a rig with limited storage than in a house.

Will a space heater work on a 15-amp campsite hookup?

Often, but with less margin than at home. A 1,500-watt heater draws roughly 12.5 amps on its own, which can leave little headroom once an air conditioner, a water heater or a refrigerator is already pulling from the same circuit. A lower-wattage heater, like a 500-watt mini drawing around 4.2 amps, leaves much more of a 15-amp circuit free for everything else running at the same time — see our full breakdown of breaker math for the household version of the same arithmetic.

How should I store a space heater between trips?

Let it cool fully before packing it away, coil the cord loosely rather than wrapping it tight around the housing, and check the intake vents for dust before the first use of the season. None of the heaters on this page is rated for damp storage, so keep it away from any spot in the rig prone to condensation.
