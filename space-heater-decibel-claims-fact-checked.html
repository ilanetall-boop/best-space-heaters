Space heater decibel claims, fact-checked

Two heaters in this small comparison print an exact decibel figure — 23 and 25. A third, priced below one of them, just says “Quiet” and leaves it there. None of the three has been measured by us, and almost nothing else in this category states a number at all. Here's what a stated dB figure is actually measuring, what it leaves out, and what the missing number on the third heater is and isn't telling you.

Quick answer

A decibel rating on a space heater box is a manufacturer's own self-reported number, measured under conditions the listing doesn't disclose — not an independently audited figure the way a window air conditioner's EnergyGuide label is. The FLANUR Smart Space Heater ($90.24) states 23 dB; the DREO 30″ Space Heater ($110.49) states 25 dB. The DREO 24″ Space Heater for Large Room ($89.00) — cheaper than the 23 dB unit and well under the 25 dB one — just says “Quiet,” with no number behind it at all.

The price doesn't track with the dB figure the way you'd expect, and a single number at idle fan speed misses most of what you'd actually notice in a bedroom at night. Read both before deciding a quieter-sounding spec sheet means a quieter room.

Why almost nothing in this catalogue prints a decibel number at all

Start with the baseline, because it reframes everything that follows: a stated decibel figure on a consumer space heater listing is the exception, not the rule. Most listings reach for an adjective instead — “quiet,” “whisper-quiet,” “silent operation” — and stop there. That's not an oversight. An adjective costs a copywriter nothing and can't be contradicted by a reader with a phone app. A number can.

Printing “23 dB” on a product title is a small, specific commitment. It implies someone put a meter somewhere near the unit, at some fan setting, at some distance, and read a figure off it. None of that process is standardized for small household appliances the way it is for, say, vehicle tire noise or hearing-protection equipment, where a published test method exists and a regulator checks it. There's no equivalent independent body certifying the noise rating on a $90 tower heater. The number is self-reported, exactly the way the wattage and the oscillation angle are self-reported — it comes from the brand's own listing, and we're repeating it here, not verifying it.

That's also why a rating's absence shouldn't be read as a confession. The DREO 24″ Space Heater for Large Room (B0FF9S4T3C, $89.00) says “Quiet” in its own title and nowhere commits to a figure. It carries a PTC ceramic element, a thermostat and remote, 70-degree oscillation, eight stated safety protections and a 12-hour timer — a full specification sheet everywhere except the one line that would let a reader compare it numerically to the other two heaters here. We're not going to fill that gap with a guess. The honest statement is that this unit's real-world loudness is simply not published anywhere we can check, which is a limitation of the listing, not a hidden defect of the heater.

It's the same structural gap we ran into with a wall heater that was the only one in its own comparison to publish a water-resistance rating: most listings in a given spec category simply don't state the number, and a reader has to decide what that silence is actually worth before assuming the unstated figure is worse than the stated one.

23dB and 25dB: what those two figures are actually measuring

The FLANUR Smart Space Heater (B0H94MPG8T, $90.24) states 23 dB directly in its title, alongside WiFi, app, voice and remote control, a 29-inch PTC ceramic tower body and 70-degree oscillation. The DREO 30″ Space Heater (B0D9PXNHL6, $110.49) states 25 dB, alongside 120-degree oscillation, an adjustable thermostat, five heat and three fan levels, and eight stated safety protections. Both numbers sit well below the roughly 40 dB most people associate with a quiet room and the 60 dB of normal conversation — general, widely published reference points for the decibel scale, not figures specific to either brand.

Two things are worth knowing about that scale before comparing 23 to 25. First, decibels are logarithmic, not linear: a jump from 23 to 25 is a genuinely small absolute difference in sound pressure, nowhere close to the perceptual gap you'd get going from, say, 30 dB to 50 dB. Read the two-point spread between these units as a minor variation, not as one heater being meaningfully louder than the other on paper. Second, neither listing tells us the fan speed, the distance from the unit, or the room conditions the figure was measured under. A dB rating almost always represents a best case — typically the lowest fan or heat setting, measured close to the unit in a quiet space — because that's the number worth printing on the box. Running either heater on its highest heat setting, with the fan pushing harder to move more air, is a different acoustic situation than whatever produced the 23 or the 25.

That caveat matters more for the FLANUR than it might first appear, because its WiFi and voice-control features suggest a unit designed to run unattended on a schedule rather than one you're standing next to while choosing a fan speed. A 23 dB figure measured at a low setting is a reasonable description of the unit idling under app control overnight; it says less about what the same tower sounds like heating a cold room from a dead start on high.

None of this means either number is dishonest. It means a stated dB figure is a data point about a specific, undisclosed test condition — useful for comparing two listings that both bother to publish one, much less useful as an absolute promise about what you'll hear in your own bedroom.

The $89 heater that just says ‘quiet,’ and what that's worth without a number

The DREO 24″ Space Heater for Large Room is the cheapest of the three heaters in this comparison at $89.00 — a dollar twenty-four less than the FLANUR's 23 dB unit, and more than twenty dollars under the 25 dB DREO. It is, by its own title, “Quiet.” It is not 23 dB, not 25 dB, not any number. It's worth being precise about what that word is and isn't worth.

What it isn't worth: evidence that the heater is louder than either of the units that do print a figure. A brand choosing not to publish a dB rating tells you about that brand's listing strategy for that specific product, not about the hardware inside the box. The same company, DREO, states a number on one of its own listings here and doesn't on the other — which is itself useful information. It shows the gap is a per-listing choice rather than a technology ceiling DREO can't clear on cheaper units. Nothing about an eight-point safety-protection list, a PTC ceramic element, a thermostat and a remote suggests a corner cut specifically on noise.

What it is worth: a genuine information gap that a shopper comparing these three heaters side by side can't close with research. “Quiet” is doing marketing work, not reporting work. It's the same category of claim as “powerful” or “fast heating” — a word chosen because it sounds reassuring and can't be checked against a competitor's identical word. If a specific noise level matters enough to your buying decision that a two-decibel gap between the other two units would sway you, this listing simply doesn't give you the input to make that comparison, and no amount of reading between the lines changes that.

Does a lower stated dB number track with a higher price here? Checking the other two

Set the unrated DREO 24″ aside for a moment and look only at the two heaters that actually publish a number, because this is where an intuitive assumption breaks. You might expect the quieter-rated unit to cost more — noise suppression sounds like an engineering feature worth paying for. That isn't what happens in this pair.

The FLANUR, rated at 23 dB — the lower, quieter figure of the two — costs $90.24. The DREO 30″, rated at 25 dB — the higher, louder figure — costs $110.49, over twenty dollars more. If dB alone set the price here, the relationship would run backwards from what it actually does: the quieter-rated heater is the cheaper one.

What actually tracks with the extra $20.25 is a longer feature list unrelated to the sound rating: the DREO 30″ adds a wider 120-degree oscillation sweep versus the FLANUR's 70 degrees, five heat levels and three fan levels instead of a simpler control scheme, and a larger 30-inch cabinet against the FLANUR's 29.1-inch body. Those are the specs that plausibly justify the price gap — size, control granularity, oscillation range — not the two-decibel difference in a stated noise figure that, as covered above, is already a minor variation on the same scale. The same pattern shows up whenever a recognizable name adds to a heater's price: the premium tends to track a bundle of features and brand reputation, not any single spec read in isolation.

The practical lesson for a shopper using dB as a tiebreaker: in this set of three, it would be a mistake to assume the pricier heater is the quieter one, or that the cheaper, unrated heater is the loudest. Price here is answering a different question than noise is.

What a decibel rating doesn't capture: the click of a thermostat, the hum through a wall

Even a trustworthy, clearly measured dB figure is describing one thing: steady-state fan noise at a given setting, measured at a given distance, under quiet lab-like conditions. It is not describing most of what actually registers as “is this heater quiet in my room” once it's running on a nightstand or against a shared wall.

What a dB number covers

Continuous airflow and motor noise at a fixed fan speed, measured near the unit in a controlled setting — the hum you'd hear standing a few feet away with nothing else running.

What it typically leaves out

The mechanical click of a relay or thermostat cycling on and off, oscillation-motor noise at the extremes of a sweep, and how low-frequency hum carries through a wall, a floor or a headboard rather than through open air.

All three heaters in this comparison use a thermostat to cycle the heating element on and off rather than running flat out continuously — the DREO 24″ has a stated thermostat and remote, the FLANUR an ECO thermostat, and the DREO 30″ an adjustable thermostat with five heat and three fan levels. Every one of those cycling events is a mechanical relay or a solid-state switch doing its job, and on plenty of consumer heaters that moment produces an audible click distinct from the fan's steady hum — a sound a single fan-noise dB rating, whatever it states, generally isn't built to capture. The same goes for two of these units' oscillation motors: the DREO 30″ sweeps a wider 120-degree arc than the FLANUR's 70 degrees, and a wider mechanical sweep is, in general, more opportunity for the motor itself to contribute a sound the fan-speed rating doesn't account for.

Then there's transmission. A heater sitting on a hard floor, a nightstand, or pushed against a shared wall can carry low-frequency hum into the structure around it in a way that has little to do with the fan's dB rating and everything to do with where the unit is physically placed. A fan is the common ingredient across nearly every heater that adds features on top of basic heat, and a fan's contribution to a quiet bedroom is as much about placement and surface contact as it is about the number printed on the box.

None of this is a claim that any of these three heaters has a thermostat click problem or a hum-through-the-wall problem — we haven't run them, and saying otherwise would be exactly the kind of invented testing claim this site doesn't make. It's a case for treating a published dB figure as one input into how a heater will actually sound in your room, not the whole answer. The same gap between a clean-sounding label and the fuller mechanism underneath it shows up with “Energy Saving” claims, which describe a control feature rather than a different heating element. A noise claim and an efficiency claim fail in the same shape: both describe a narrow, favorable slice of real operation and let the reader assume it's the whole picture.

Reading a ‘quiet’ claim when there's no number behind it

The practical takeaway from all three listings isn't “always buy the one with a number” — it's a short set of questions worth running before a stated or unstated noise claim moves your decision.

  • Is there a number at all, or just an adjective? “Quiet,” “whisper-quiet” and “silent operation” are marketing language with no measurement behind them. A specific dB figure is a stronger claim, even an unverified one, simply because it can theoretically be checked against another product's stated figure.
  • Does the listing say what the number was measured under? None of the three listings here states the fan speed, heat setting or distance used to produce 23 dB or 25 dB. Assume any printed figure represents the gentlest, most favorable setting the unit has, not its full-output noise level.
  • Is the price premium actually buying quiet, or buying something else? In this comparison, the extra cost between the 23 dB and 25 dB units tracked oscillation range and control granularity, not the noise rating. Check what else changed before crediting a price gap to sound alone.
  • Does the gap even matter on the decibel scale? A two-point spread like 23 to 25 is small, given how the scale works. Don't treat a minor printed difference as a meaningful real-world one.
  • What does the number leave out? A steady fan-noise rating says nothing about a thermostat's click, an oscillation motor's own sound, or how hum carries through a wall or floor where the unit sits. Placement will do as much work as the spec sheet.

None of this is a reason to distrust any of the three heaters in this comparison specifically. It's a reason to read a decibel figure, or the absence of one, as a data point about the listing rather than a verdict on the hardware — and to remember that the quietest-sounding claim on the page and the quietest heater in your actual bedroom aren't guaranteed to be the same product.

Frequently asked questions

Why do only some space heaters print a decibel number?

Because there's no independent standard or regulator requiring it for this category, publishing a dB figure is a voluntary, self-reported choice by the brand, and most listings use an adjective like “quiet” instead because it costs nothing to claim and can't be directly checked against a competitor's number. A stated figure is a stronger claim than an adjective, but it's still unverified by anyone outside the manufacturer.

Is 23 dB meaningfully quieter than 25 dB?

Only marginally. The decibel scale is logarithmic, so a two-point gap like this is a small absolute difference, well inside the kind of variation you'd expect from measuring at slightly different fan speeds or distances. Both figures sit far below typical conversation-level noise, and neither listing discloses the exact conditions the number was measured under, which matters more than the two-point spread itself.

Does the DREO heater without a stated dB number perform worse than the ones that publish a figure?

There's no evidence either way. The DREO 24″ Space Heater for Large Room doesn't state a decibel figure, but it's priced below one of the two heaters that does, and the same brand publishes a number on its own 30-inch model. That shows the gap is a listing choice rather than proof of a louder unit. Treat the missing number as unverifiable, not as a worse result.

Does a lower stated dB rating mean a higher price?

Not in this comparison. The heater with the lower, quieter stated figure (23 dB, $90.24) actually costs less than the one with the higher figure (25 dB, $110.49). The price gap between those two tracks a wider oscillation range and more heat and fan settings, not the noise rating itself.
