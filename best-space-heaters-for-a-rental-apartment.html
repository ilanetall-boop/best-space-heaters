The best space heaters for a rental apartment

You can't repaint the walls, you can't swap the thermostat, and the security deposit depends on returning the unit exactly as you found it. A space heater is one of the few ways a renter gets to control their own warmth — but the brief is different from a homeowner's: nothing permanent, nothing risky, nothing that doesn't fit back in a moving box. We compared three plug-in heaters built around that constraint, on published specs and current prices, not on how big a number they claim.

Why a rental changes the brief

A homeowner shopping for a space heater is really asking one question: how much of this room can it heat? A renter is asking at least three more, and all of them involve someone else's property. Can I install it without touching a wall, a breaker box or anything the lease calls a "fixture"? Will it survive being packed into a box three times before the lease is up? And if it fails, breaks, or simply isn't needed anymore, is the money lost small enough that it doesn't matter?

None of that shows up on a spec sheet, but it decides which heaters belong on this page and which don't. A built-in wall unit, a hardwired baseboard heater, anything that requires an electrician or a hole in drywall is off the table by definition — a rental agreement that says nothing explicit about portable heat almost always says something explicit about alterations, and a space heater's entire appeal is that it plugs into an existing outlet and leaves nothing behind when it's unplugged. Every heater on this page is exactly that: a free-standing unit that arrives in a box, runs off a standard wall outlet, and leaves with you.

The three picks below also share something a homeowner might not prioritize: low enough cost that replacing one, or simply not taking it to the next apartment, is a shrug rather than a decision. At $20.69, $24.99 and $44.99, none of these three is a purchase you need to defend to a future version of yourself in a city you haven't moved to yet.

Overheat and tip-over protection: the non-negotiable, not the bonus

In a house you own, a space heater that tips over is your problem and your floor. In a rental, it's also your landlord's floor, your downstairs neighbor's ceiling, and very possibly a line item in whatever renters insurance you were told to buy before you got the keys. That's why automatic shut-off on tip-over, and a cutoff if the unit overheats, aren't nice extras here — they're the first thing worth checking, ahead of wattage, ahead of noise, ahead of how it looks on a shelf.

Of the three heaters here, the Amazon Basics Ceramic Space Heater is the one that states this outright: its own product title includes "With Overheat and Tip-Over Protection," which means the manufacturer is making that claim directly rather than leaving it implied. The AUBKN and JIBUFI listings we have don't spell out a tip-over or overheat feature in the title or specification data provided to us, and we're not going to credit either one with a safety feature its own listing doesn't state. That's not a claim either heater lacks the feature — plenty of small ceramic and PTC heaters in this price range include it as standard — it's a gap in the data we were given. If a specific certification matters to you, especially in a building where you don't control the wiring or the smoke detectors, read the safety-feature bullets on the current listing before you buy, not after.

Footprint: what fits in a closet when the lease ends

A space heater that lives in your apartment for eighteen months also has to live in a box, a trunk, or the back of a closet for however many months it isn't plugged in — between leases, during a move, or simply in summer. That makes footprint a rental-specific spec in a way it usually isn't for a house: the question isn't just "does it fit the room," it's "does it fit the storage I actually have," which in most rentals is a shelf, not a basement. The outlet you have, not the one you wish you had

A homeowner can call an electrician. A renter works with the outlets the apartment already has, in wiring that might predate anyone currently living there. That matters because a 1,500-watt heater draws roughly 12.5 amps, close to the working limit of a standard 15-amp household circuit, and older buildings — the kind that make up a large share of the rental stock in most US cities — are exactly where circuits are more likely to be shared across more than one room, or where a single bathroom and bedroom outlet trace back to the same breaker. Our guide on matching wattage to your room goes through the arithmetic in full.

The JIBUFI and Amazon Basics listings both state 1,500 watts directly. The AUBKN listing we have does not state a wattage figure; treat 1,500 watts as the reasonable working assumption for a plug-in heater of this type and price unless its own listing says otherwise, and don't assume a lower number just because the box doesn't print one. Whichever heater you choose, the practical rental habit is the same: plug it straight into a wall outlet, never into a shared extension cord or a power strip already carrying a lamp and a space-constrained kitchen's worth of small appliances, and if a breaker trips the moment the heater and something else switch on together, that's the circuit telling you it's shared — not a fault with the heater. If your building allows it, confirm with the landlord or a prior tenant which outlets in the unit share a breaker before you settle on where the heater lives.

What to photograph before you plug one in, for the walkthrough

The move-out walkthrough is where a space heater can quietly cost a renter part of a deposit, not because the heater caused damage but because nobody can prove it didn't. A heating element run for months in the same spot can leave a faint discoloration on carpet or a baseboard, a cord pinched under a rug can leave an indentation, and an outlet that already had a hairline crack in its faceplate before you moved in looks exactly like one you caused if there's no record otherwise. None of this requires the heater to have done anything wrong — it requires you to have no proof of the apartment's condition before you started using it.

Before you plug a heater in for the first time in a new place, a few photos take less time than finding the box it came in:

  • The outlet itself. A close, well-lit photo of the faceplate, front-on, showing any existing cracks, scorch marks or discoloration around the slots.
  • The floor and baseboard where the heater will sit. Carpet, hardwood or vinyl, photographed before the heater's feet or cord have touched it, so any pre-existing stain, scratch or warp is on record as pre-existing.
  • The wall directly behind and above where the heater will run, especially if it's near a curtain, bedding or furniture, since radiant heat left running for a long time can faintly discolor paint over months.
  • Any extension cord, power strip or outlet cover already installed in that spot, so you have a record of what you found versus what you added or removed.

None of this is about proving a heater is safe — the tip-over and overheat features covered above do that job. It's about proving what the apartment already looked like, which is the one piece of evidence a landlord can't dispute with photos of their own, because you took them first.

Frequently asked questions

Will my landlord or lease care about a portable space heater?

Most leases don't single out portable electric heaters the way they single out pets or subletting, because a plug-in heater isn't an alteration to the unit — it doesn't touch wiring, walls or fixtures. That said, lease terms vary by building and by state, and some do restrict space-heater use for fire-safety reasons, particularly in older buildings. Read your own lease for any clause mentioning portable heating or fire hazards, and when in doubt, a short message to the landlord or property manager costs nothing and settles the question for good.

What should I photograph before I plug one in, for the move-out walkthrough?

The outlet faceplate, the floor and baseboard where the heater will sit, and the wall behind it, all photographed before the heater has run even once. The goal is a record of what the apartment looked like before you added anything, so any pre-existing mark can't later be attributed to the heater.

Will a space heater trip the breaker in an older building?

It can, more often in older buildings than new ones, because a 1,500-watt heater draws roughly 12.5 amps against a standard 15-amp circuit's rating, and older wiring is more likely to share a single circuit across more than one room. Plugging the heater directly into its own wall outlet, rather than an extension cord shared with other appliances, is the single habit that prevents most of these trips.

Can I take the heater with me when I move out?

Yes — every heater on this page is a free-standing, plug-in appliance, not a fixture, so it belongs to you and leaves when you do. That's part of why footprint and price matter so much for a rental pick: a heater that packs flat into a box and didn't cost much to begin with is one less thing to think twice about when the lease is up.
